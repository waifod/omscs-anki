## Front matter (title, abstract, authors)
- The pull-quote thesis: the authors survey current DSM approaches and conclude that the reduced cost of parallel software development will help make the DSM paradigm a viable solution to large-scale, high-performance computing. (cover)
- Publication data: IEEE 1063-6552/96, copyright 1996 IEEE, "Summer 1996", first body page numbered 63. (p.63)
- All three authors are at the School of Electrical Engineering, University of Belgrade. Protic was a PhD student working on DSM; Tomasevic and Milutinovic held BSc/MSc/PhD in the field. Milutinovic is noted as a coarchitect of one of the first 200-MHz microprocessors with over 40 IEEE journal papers. (bios, p.79)
- The paper positions itself as "not exhaustive" but an "extensive, up-to-date overview of several key implementation schemes". (p.64)

## Shared-memory vs distributed-memory background (the dichotomy the lesson skips)
- The paper opens with a two-way classification the lesson does not state: shared-memory systems (tightly coupled multiprocessors) with a global physical memory equally accessible to all processors, versus distributed-memory systems (multicomputers) with independent nodes, local memory, and a general interconnection network. (p.63)
- Shared-memory systems give a convenient uniform read/write programming model and cut software development cost, but suffer contention, longer latencies, limited scalability, and complex memory-system design. (p.63)
- Distributed-memory systems scale well but force a message-passing model with explicit send/receive, make the programmer handle data distribution and communication, and complicate process migration because of distinct address spaces. (p.63)
- The paper's one-line summary of the trade-off: compared with shared-memory systems, "hardware problems are easier and software problems more complex in distributed-memory systems". DSM exists to combine the two: it logically implements shared memory on a physically distributed system, hiding the remote-communication mechanism. (p.63)
- DSM inherits the shared-memory programming ease and portability (preserving software investment) and the scalability and cost-effectiveness of distributed memory. (p.63)

## General DSM system structure
- A DSM system is a set of nodes or clusters on an interconnection network; a cluster can itself be a uniprocessor or a bus-organized multiprocessor, and private caches on the processors are "virtually inevitable" for reducing latency. (p.64, Figure 1)
- Each cluster's local memory maps partially or entirely into the DSM global address space. A per-cluster interconnection controller attaches it to the network regardless of topology (bus, ring, mesh, or LAN). (p.64)
- State and current location of data blocks live in a system table or **directory**. Directory storage and organization is "among the most important design decisions, greatly affecting system scalability". Organizations range from full-map to dynamic structures (single/double-linked lists, trees), and the directory can be flat or hierarchical, centralized or distributed across clusters. (p.64)
- In hierarchical topologies, intermediate-level clusters may hold only directories and interface controllers. Directory semantics depend on the consistency-maintenance method used. (p.64)

## The three design axes
- The paper organizes the whole field around three questions the lesson touches only loosely (p.64):
  - How the access executes -> the **DSM algorithm**.
  - Where the mechanism is implemented -> the **implementation level**.
  - What "consistent" means -> the **memory consistency model**.

## DSM algorithms (the lesson's "access algorithm" section, in far more depth)
- The two basic problems a DSM algorithm solves: static and dynamic distribution of shared data to minimize access latency, and preserving a coherent view while minimizing coherence overhead. (p.65)
- Replication vs migration defined precisely (p.65): replication allows multiple copies in different memories/caches, used mainly for simultaneous reads when read sharing prevails; migration keeps a single copy that must be moved to the requesting site for exclusive use, preferred when sequential write-sharing patterns prevail. The lesson ties migration to single reader/single writer; the paper ties it to write-sharing locality. [The migration definition and the write-sharing preference are now stated in the note's Protic callout (distributed-shared-memory.md, section DSM Design: Migration vs. Replication); the replication definition is not.]
- The classification follows Stumm and Zhou. (p.65)

### Single reader/single writer (SRSW)
- Prohibits replication; permits but does not require migration. (p.65) [That SRSW prohibits replication is now stated in the note's Protic callout (distributed-shared-memory.md, section DSM Design: Access Algorithm); that migration is permitted but not required is not.]
- The simplest algorithm is the **central server**: one server services all requests to data physically on it; it is a bottleneck, implies no physical distribution. A modification statically distributes memory and server responsibility, using a mapping function such as hashing to locate the server. (p.65)
- Migration-capable SRSW is the **hot potato** algorithm: still only one copy, which migrates on demand; data moves in fixed-size blocks so migration cost amortizes over multiple accesses under high reference locality. Performance is "restrictively low" because it cannot exploit parallel read-only copies; the paper calls it "rarely used". (p.65) [The name, single migrating copy, and fixed-block amortization are now stated in the note's Protic callout (distributed-shared-memory.md, section DSM Design: Migration vs. Replication); the "restrictively low" performance and "rarely used" verdict are not.]

### Multiple reader/single writer (MRSW)
- Also called **read-replication**; the goal is reducing the average read cost since read sharing dominates. Simultaneous reads at multiple hosts; only one host at a time may update, and a write to a writable copy is costly because stale copies must be prevented from use, so MRSW algorithms are "usually invalidation-based". (p.65-66) [Read-replication and the invalidation basis (stale copies must be prevented) are now stated in the note's Protic callout (distributed-shared-memory.md, section DSM Design: Access Algorithm); the read-cost goal is not.]
- Three terms defined (p.66): **manager** (site responsible for organizing write access to a block), **owner** (site owning the only writable copy), **copy set** (all sites holding copies). The lesson uses home/owner but not "copy set".
- The five Li and Hudak algorithms, which the lesson does not enumerate (p.66):
  1. **Centralized manager**: all read/write requests go to the manager (the only site knowing the owner); it forwards to the owner, waits for confirmation, and for writes sends invalidations to every site in the copy set.
  2. **Improved centralized manager**: the owner, not the manager, keeps the copy set, which travels with the data to the new owner, who performs invalidations; decentralized synchronization improves performance.
  3. **Fixed distributed manager**: each site manages a predetermined subset of blocks by a default mapping (overridable by clients); outperforms centralized solutions under a high request rate.
  4. **Broadcast distributed manager**: no manager; a requester broadcasts to find the true owner, which acts as the manager and keeps the copy set; every processor must process each broadcast, slowing its own work.
  5. **Dynamic distributed manager** ("Li's algorithm"): each block tracks a *probable* owner rather than the real one; requests chase the probable-owner chain, and probable-owner fields update on every request/forward/invalidation; performance degrades only logarithmically as processors contend. A tree-structured copy-set variant distributes invalidation responsibility.

### Multiple reader/multiple writer (MRMW)
- Also called **full-replication**; allows replicated blocks with read and write permission, updates distributed to all copies by multicast/broadcast; appropriate for write sharing, often with write-update protocols; can produce high coherence traffic when update frequency and replica count are high. (p.66) [Full-replication, read-write replicas, multicast/broadcast updates, and write-update protocols are now stated in the note's Protic callout (distributed-shared-memory.md, section DSM Design: Access Algorithm); the high-coherence-traffic caveat is not.]
- One coherence technique: globally sequence writes via reliable multicast through a **sequencer** that assigns sequence numbers; a site that receives an out-of-order modification requests retransmission. A distributed-sequencer variant has the master-copy server sequence writes per data structure; the system is then not sequentially consistent, but each structure stays individually consistent. (p.66-67)

## Avenues for performance improvement (entirely absent from the lesson)
- Research modifications reduce transferred data; most were evaluated by simulation, some implemented. (p.67)
- **Shrewd** algorithm: an enhancement of Li's algorithm that eliminates unnecessary page transfers using a per-copy sequence number; on a write fault at a node with a read-only copy, if its sequence number matches the owner's the page need not be transferred; large benefit as the read-to-write ratio rises. (p.67)
- **Competitive** replication/migration: replicate a remote page only when accesses exceed replication cost; migration has a stricter, access-pattern-dependent condition; performance guaranteed within a constant factor of optimal. (p.67)
- **Mirage** time window: defines a window of duration delta during which a site is guaranteed to hold a page uninterruptedly after acquiring it, tunable statically or dynamically, to curb **thrashing** (the ping-pong of migration under alternating accesses from different sites). (p.67) [Now stated in the note's Protic callout (distributed-shared-memory.md, section DSM Design: Migration vs. Replication), so not beyond the notes.]
- The paper's note on evolution: early DSM found gains in table organization (copy set) and management distribution; recent DSM relaxes consistency semantics, uses hardware accelerators, and combines invalidate and update methods. (p.67)

## Implementation level (software / hardware / hybrid)
- Every access needs a lookup to decide whether data is local, and a write needs an action to preserve coherence; both can run in software, hardware, or a combination, giving three groups. The lesson gives only hardware vs software; the paper adds hybrid as a first-class category. (p.67) [The hybrid third category is now stated in the note's Protic callout (distributed-shared-memory.md, section Hardware vs. Software DSM); the lookup and coherence-action framing is not.]
- Choice is a price/performance trade-off: hardware is faster but complex and affordable only for high-performance large-scale machines; low-end commodity systems are limited to software; mid-range clusters of workstations suit low-cost hybrid solutions. (p.67) [Now stated in the note's Protic callout (distributed-shared-memory.md, section Hardware vs. Software DSM), so not beyond the notes.]

### Software DSM implementations (none of these systems are in the lesson)
- Software is more flexible and tailors consistency to application behavior, but usually cannot match hardware performance; relaxed consistency helps but burdens the programmer. Larger grain sizes (around a kilobyte) are typical because management is done through virtual memory and page faults. Coarse pages help high-locality applications and cut directory storage, but hurt fine-grain-sharing programs through false sharing and thrashing. (p.68) [Directory-storage savings, the high-locality benefit, and false sharing plus thrashing as the coarse-grain hazards are now stated in the note's Protic callout (distributed-shared-memory.md, section DSM Design: Sharing Granularity); the flexibility trade-off and the kilobyte grain via virtual memory are not.]
- User-level and combined implementations surveyed in the sidebar: IVY, Mermaid, Munin, TreadMarks, Midway, Blizzard, Mirage, Clouds, Orca, Linda (Table A gives type, algorithm, consistency model, granularity, coherence policy). (sidebar p.68-70)
  - **IVY**: one of the first software DSM systems; user-level modules on a modified Aegis OS on Apollo Domain workstations; invalidation on 1-Kbyte pages; implements three sequential-consistency algorithms (improved centralized, fixed distributed, dynamic distributed managers); near-linear speedup to eight clusters; its contribution was proving DSM viable. (p.68)
  - **Mermaid**: first heterogeneous DSM (HDSM); Sun/Unix workstations plus DEC Firefly multiprocessors; user-level library plus minor SunOS kernel changes; needs data-conversion routines for heterogeneity; one data type per page; variable page size; comparing against the Firefly's physically shared memory, speedup increased by less than 20% when moving from DSM to physically shared memory, for up to four nodes. (p.68)
  - **Munin**: type-specific coherence plus release consistency; Sun-3 Ethernet, Unix System V + Presto; runtime system + preprocessor + modified linker + library + OS support; programmer annotates each shared object with an access-pattern type (Table B: read-only, migratory, write-shared, producer-consumer, etc.) selecting a coherence protocol; within 10% of hand-coded message passing. (p.68-69, Table B)
  - **TreadMarks**: lazy release consistency; user-level, Unix standard libraries, no kernel or compiler changes; multiple-writer invalidation using a twin/diff scheme (copy a page on first write, diff later); good speedups for five Splash programs on DECstation-5000/240 over ATM and Ethernet. (p.69)
  - **Midway**: supports multiple consistency models (processor, release, entry) switchable dynamically; MIPS R3000 DEC stations under Mach; shared data explicitly associated with a synchronization object; sequential consistency available if all shared accesses are properly labelled; update mechanism; near-linear speedups on ATM. (p.69-70)
  - **Blizzard**: user-level + OS kernel modification, built on the Tempest interface; three variants, Blizzard-S (software, insert a check routine before each shared reference), Blizzard-E (uses ECC bits to mark invalid blocks, MMU page protection for read-only), Blizzard-ES (combination); Blizzard-S most efficient (within a factor of two); slowdown of several times vs the KSR1 hardware DSM. (p.70)
  - **Mirage** (OS-kernel implementation): coherence inside the kernel; VAX 11/750 over Ethernet, System V interface; main contribution is the time-window D guaranteeing page ownership to avoid thrashing; page-segmentation model; all requests go to the segment-creation "library site" and queue sequentially; a "Clock site" supplies the most recent copy. (p.70, Figure A)
  - **Clouds**: OS-based DSM outside the Ra kernel; Sun-3 over Ethernet; objects of segments with access attributes read-only/read-write/weak-read/none; weak-read gives a copy with no guarantee against concurrent modification, so unrestricted use leads to inconsistent DSM; get/discard fetch primitives and P/V semaphores from a DSM controller (DSMC). (p.70-71)
  - **Linda**: DSM as a "tuple space", a pool of user-defined tuples addressed by logical name, with operators to insert/remove/read tuples; avoids the consistency problem by removing a tuple before update and reinserting the modified version; implemented on shared-memory and loosely coupled systems. (sidebar p.71)
  - **Orca**: Bal and Tanenbaum's shared-data-object model (passive objects accessed through predefined operations) for distributed programming; selective replication, migration, and update; variant update mechanisms depending on the underlying communication (point-to-point, reliable/unreliable multicast). (sidebar p.71) [OCR renders Tanenbaum as "Tannenbaum".]

### Hardware DSM implementations (the lesson's hardware section, taxonomized)
- Hardware DSM replicates shared data in local memories and caches transparently to software, supporting fine-grain sharing at cache-line granularity; it extends scalable cache-coherence schemes, reduces communication and the effects of false sharing/thrashing, and speeds search/directory functions, at the cost of design and verification complexity. (p.69) [Cache-line granularity as an extension of cache coherence, reduced false sharing and thrashing, faster search and directory functions, and design/verification complexity are now stated in the note's Protic callout (distributed-shared-memory.md, section Hardware vs. Software DSM); transparent replication and reduced communication are not.]
- Three families (hardware sidebar, Table C, p.72-75): [The three families and their example systems are now stated in the note's Protic callout (distributed-shared-memory.md, section Hardware vs. Software DSM); the per-family and per-system details below are not.]
  - **CC-NUMA** (cache-coherent non-uniform memory architecture): shared virtual address space statically distributed across local memories, accessible by all but with non-uniform latency; directory-based (full-map to dynamic); invalidation-based coherence; relaxed models for performance. Representatives:
    - **Memnet**: ring-based "Memory as Network Abstraction"; address space maps onto a reserved area of each local memory NUMA-style, plus a cache area for 32-byte remote blocks; coherence in hardware state machines; ring snooping satisfies misses from the nearest valid copy. (p.72-73)
    - **Dash**: Directory Architecture for Shared Memory; 4-processor clusters, each owning part of memory and directory; two-level private caches hold remote 16-byte blocks; four-level memory hierarchy; full-map directory with block states uncached/cached/dirty; release consistency plus prefetch/update/deliver optimizations; hardware synchronization support. (p.73)
    - **SCI**: the IEEE P1596 Scalable Coherent Interface standard (an interface standard, not a full design); distributes the directory among sharing caches as doubly linked lists; home keeps only a head pointer and status bits; read misses chain the requester as the new list head; writes invalidate down the list; proposed enhancements include sharing trees, request combining, queue-based locks. (p.73-74)
  - **COMA** (cache-only memory architecture): local memories act as huge caches ("attraction memories") with no fixed home; data replicated/migrated on demand; hierarchical network topology simplifies locating blocks and replacement; less sensitive to static data distribution than NUMA but higher remote-miss latency and storage overhead. Representatives:
    - **KSR1**: ring-based hierarchy, 32-Mbyte local cache; allocation unit a 16-Kbyte page, transfer/sharing unit a 128-byte subpage; the "Allcache engine" routes requests through the hierarchy; invalidation protocol with states exclusive/nonexclusive/copy/invalid plus an atomic state for synchronization; cache-line replacement is complicated by having no main-memory home. (p.74)
    - **DDM**: Data Diffusion Machine; 4-processor clusters with attraction memory and a split-transaction bus; set-associative directory holding state (no data) for items below it; snoopy write-invalidate; seven item states (invalid/exclusive/valid plus four transient). (p.74)
  - **RMS** (reflective memory system): hardware update at fine granularity; shared segments mapped through programmable tables and selectively "reflected" across clusters; full-replication MRMW with each write broadcast/multicast to all clusters holding the address; processor does not stall on writes; all reads are local with deterministic time; resembles write-update cache coherence. Representatives:
    - **RMS** (Encore): bus-based, a write-only RM bus carrying word-based writes (address + value); later Memory Channel adds block updates; 8-Kbyte replication segment treated as a window that can be open (reflected) or closed. (p.74-75)
    - **Merlin**: mesh-based reflective memory with word-granularity sharing; OS calls initialize routing maps; the interface noninvasively copies each shared write and multicasts it; two sharing modes, synchronous (through a canonical cluster) and rapid (shortest routes); supports heterogeneous processing. (p.75)

### Hybrid-level DSM implementations (whole category absent from the lesson)
- Even "entirely hardware" systems expose software-controlled features (prefetch/update/deliver in Dash, prefetch/poststore in KSR1), and "purely software" systems need hardware support (VM hardware in IVY, ECC in Blizzard-E); hence hybrids balance cost and complexity. This is the material the lesson quiz draws on. (p.70-71) [Now stated in the note's Protic callout (distributed-shared-memory.md, section Hardware vs. Software DSM), so not beyond the notes.]
- Systems (Table D, p.76-77):
  - **Plus**: software data placement/replacement in 4-Kbyte pages; hardware 32-bit-word write-update coherence; replicas chained in a singly linked list headed by the master; nonblocking writes with a user "fence" for strong ordering; "delayed operations" split read-modify-write into issue/verify phases. (p.76) [The software replication/migration versus hardware coherence split is now stated in the note's Protic callout (distributed-shared-memory.md, section Hardware vs. Software DSM); the page and word sizes, replica list, fence, and delayed operations are not.]
  - **Galactica Net**: on-demand page replication under Mach VM software plus a hardware block-transfer engine; page states read-only/private/update; hardware write-update over a "virtual sharing ring"; can switch dynamically from hardware update to software invalidate using per-page update counters when remote updates dominate. (p.76)
  - **MIT Alewife**: the LimitLESS directory protocol; hardware directory entries hold a limited number of pointers, with software (via interrupt and fast trap) emulating a full-map directory in the rare overflow cases; multiple-context concept hides latency. (p.76-77)
  - **Flash**: software coherence protocol executed on an auxiliary "Magic" protocol processor rather than the main CPU, pipelined, avoiding main-processor context switches; also supports low-overhead message passing. (p.77)
  - **Typhoon**: proposed hardware for the Tempest interface (low-overhead messages, bulk transfer, VM management, fine-grain access control); a network-interface processor dispatches user-level handlers; "Stache" user library replicates remote data COMA-style at page granularity, coherence at block level. (p.77)
  - **Shrimp**: reflective memory with a virtual-memory-mapped network interface doing automatic update in hardware; AURC (automatic update release consistency) keeps one copy consistent by fine-grain automatic update and others by software invalidation; also uses "scope consistency", a compromise between entry and lazy release consistency. (p.77)
- Other hybrid approaches (p.77):
  - **Cooperative shared memory / CICO**: programmer Check-In/Check-Out annotations as performance primitives that do not change semantics even if misapplied; supported by a minimal directory protocol Dir1SW. [OCR renders this as "Dir, SW".]
  - A hybrid protocol combining a Midway-like software part for coarse-grain regions (BeginRead/EndRead, BeginWrite/EndWrite annotations) with directory-based hardware for fine-grain, both invalidation-based; variable-size software coherence unit eliminates false sharing.
  - Convergence of message passing and shared memory: Alewife allows explicit message sends in a shared-memory program; Flash adds low-overhead message passing with protection.

## Memory consistency models (the section to check against the lesson hardest)
- Definition: the consistency model defines the legal ordering of memory references issued by a processor as observed by other processors; stronger models raise latency and bandwidth needs but simplify programming, looser models improve performance at the cost of programmer effort to synchronize. (p.71)
- The paper divides models into those that treat synchronization accesses as ordinary (sequential, processor) and those that distinguish them (weak, release, lazy release, entry). Its taxonomy has **no strict and no causal consistency**; those two models in the lesson come from the general distributed-systems literature (Tanenbaum), not from this paper. (p.71-72) [The absence of strict and causal consistency is now stated in the note's Protic callout (distributed-shared-memory.md, section Strict Consistency) and the note's Protic callout (distributed-shared-memory.md, section Causal Consistency); the split by treatment of synchronization accesses is not.]
- **Sequential** (Lamport): all processors observe the same interleaving of reads/writes issued in sequences by individual processors; a simple implementation is a single-port memory serializing a FIFO queue, or a DSM central server serializing requests, with no bypassing of same-processor requests; holds in most bus-based multiprocessors and early DSM (IVY, Mirage). (p.72-73) [The same-interleaving definition is now stated in the note's Protic callout (distributed-shared-memory.md, section Sequential Consistency); the implementations and where it holds are not.]
- **Processor** (not in the lesson): processors need not see an identical order of all operations, but all must see each processor's own writes in that processor's issue order; reads may bypass writes in the service queue. Examples: VAX 8800, Plus, Merlin, RMS. (p.73-74) [Now stated in the note's Protic callout (distributed-shared-memory.md, section Sequential Consistency), so not beyond the notes.]
- **Weak**: distinguishes ordinary from synchronization accesses; memory need be consistent only on synchronization accesses, and sequential-consistency requirements apply only to the synchronization accesses themselves; a synchronization access waits for all previous accesses to complete, while ordinary reads/writes wait only for completion of previous synchronization accesses. Sun SPARC uses a variant. The lesson describes the visibility effect but not the requirement that syncs are sequentially consistent among themselves. (p.74) [Now stated in the note's Protic callout (distributed-shared-memory.md, section Weak Consistency), so not beyond the notes.]
- **Release**: splits synchronization into acquire and release; ordinary accesses may run only after prior acquires on the same processor, a release only after prior ordinary reads/writes complete, and acquire/release accesses must meet processor-consistency requirements. Dash and Munin implement it differently. This is the acquire/release pair the lesson calls "entry/acquire" and "exit/release". (p.74) [The acquire/release ordering rules are now stated in the note's Protic callout (distributed-shared-memory.md, section Weak Consistency) and the Dash/Munin examples in the note's Protic callout (distributed-shared-memory.md, section DSM Design: Consistency Management); the processor-consistency requirement on acquire/release accesses is not.]
- **Lazy release**: enhancement of release; propagates modifications at the next relevant acquire rather than at each release (release is "sometimes called eager consistency"), and only those modifications in the chain of preceding synchronization operations on that specific lock, cutting data and message counts. TreadMarks implements it. (p.75) [Now stated in the note's Protic callout (distributed-shared-memory.md, section DSM Design: Consistency Management), so not beyond the notes.]
- **Entry**: improves release consistency by requiring every ordinary shared variable/object to be associated with a synchronization variable, so an acquire propagates only the subset that variable guards; lower latency because a shared access need not wait on unrelated acquires; higher programmer burden. Midway first implemented it. The lesson blurs entry with release. (p.77) [The per-variable synchronization association, subset propagation, and Midway are now stated in the note's Protic callout (distributed-shared-memory.md, section Weak Consistency); the latency benefit and programmer burden are not.]

## Important design choices (a checklist the lesson gives only piecemeal)
- Beyond the three axes, the paper lists characteristics that strongly affect performance (p.78): cluster configuration, interconnection network, structure of shared data, coherence-unit granularity, DSM-management responsibility, and coherence policy.
- **Cluster configuration**: off-the-shelf processors with their own caches, so cluster-level cache coherence must integrate with the DSM mechanism; local memory split private/shared; the NIC sometimes carries DSM management duties. (p.78)
- **Interconnection network**: most software DSM is network-independent (often Ethernet); hardware DSM uses multilevel buses, ring hierarchies, meshes; topology affects parallel data exchange, scalability, and the cost of broadcast/multicast (important for the algorithms). (p.78)
- **Shared data structure**: hardware deals with non-structured objects; software uses logical data items to exploit application locality. (p.78)
- **Coherence-unit granularity**: hardware uses small units (cache blocks), software uses larger ones (pages); larger blocks save directory storage but raise false sharing and thrashing. [OCR garbles "thrashing" as "thrsehily" here.] (p.78) [Now stated in the note's Protic callout (distributed-shared-memory.md, section Hardware vs. Software DSM), the note's Protic callout (distributed-shared-memory.md, section DSM Design: Sharing Granularity), and the note body (distributed-shared-memory.md, section DSM Design: Sharing Granularity), so not beyond the notes.]
- **DSM management responsibility**: centralized (easy but a bottleneck) vs distributed (static or dynamic, scalable), closely tied to directory distribution. (p.78)
- **Coherence policy**: update vs invalidate, chosen by granularity; for very fine-grain data an update costs about the same as an invalidation, so word-based systems favour update and coarse-grain systems favour invalidation; invalidation is better when read/write sequences to a datum are not highly interleaved; best is a policy that adapts dynamically to reference patterns. (p.78-79)

## Conclusion and research directions
- DSM is a viable solution for large-scale high-performance systems with reduced parallel-software cost, but commercial systems are "in their infancy" and research prototypes still prevail. (p.79)
- Promising directions listed: improving/adapting algorithms to applications and configurations; synergistic hardware-software combination; integrating shared-memory and message-passing paradigms; new memory-system architectures; and combining multiple consistency models. (p.79)
- Acknowledgments: partly funded by the National Science Foundation of Serbia and National Technology Foundation of Serbia; thanks to Vojislav Protic and Liviu Iftode. (p.79)

## Notes vs paper discrepancies
Each item was checked against the paper text and at least one third-party source; sources are under "Discrepancy sources".

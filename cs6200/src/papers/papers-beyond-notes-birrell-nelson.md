# Birrell & Nelson: everything not in the notes

## Abstract and front matter
- The paper describes a specific RPC package built at Xerox PARC, the design options its authors faced, and the decisions they made; it covers structure, binding, the transport-level protocol, performance measurements, and optimizations to minimize load on servers with many clients. (abstract, p.39)
- CR categories: C.2.2 (Network Protocols: protocol architecture), C.2.4 (Distributed Systems: distributed applications, network operating systems), D.4.4 (Operating Systems: Communications Management), D.4.7 (Operating Systems: Organization and Design). General terms: Design, Experimentation, Performance, Security. Additional keywords include transport layer protocols, distributed naming and binding, interprocess communication, performance of communication protocols. (abstract, p.39)
- Authors' address: Xerox Palo Alto Research Center, 3333 Coyote Hill Road, Palo Alto, CA 94304. Copyright 1984 ACM. (p.39)

## Section 1.1: Background and introduction
- The idea of RPC extends the within-a-program control-and-data-transfer mechanism of procedure call across a communication network: the caller is suspended, parameters are passed to the callee's environment, the procedure runs there, and results come back as from an ordinary return. While the caller is suspended, other processes on its machine may still run, depending on the parallelism of the environment. (p.39-40) [Partly stated in the note body (remote-procedure-calls.md, section RPC Requirements): the synchronous semantics, with the calling process (or at least the calling thread) blocking until the procedure returns, as in a local call; the parameter passing into the callee's environment and the parallelism remark remain beyond the notes.]
- Three attractions of the idea: clean and simple semantics; efficiency (procedure calls seem simple enough for communication to be rapid); and generality (procedures are the main intra-machine communication mechanism). (p.40)
- The idea had been discussed in the literature since at least 1976; Nelson's doctoral dissertation is an extensive examination of the design space. Full-scale implementations were rarer than paper designs; notable efforts were Courier (Xerox NS protocol family) and work at MIT. (p.40)
- The paper's work was done for the Cedar project; the authors believed they understood the designer's choices from earlier work, especially Nelson's thesis, but found several areas inadequately understood and produced a design with several novel aspects. (p.40)
- The major issues facing an RPC designer, listed: precise semantics under machine and communication failures; semantics of address-containing arguments without a shared address space; integration into existing/future programming systems; binding (how a caller finds the callee's location and identity); transfer protocols for data and control; data integrity and security on an open network. (p.40)
- The paper defers encryption-based security and the detailed manufacture of stub modules to planned later papers. (p.40)

## Section 1.2: Environment
- Cedar is a large project to build a powerful, convenient programming environment with uniform, highly interactive user interfaces, for single-user workstations and also for building servers. (p.40-41)
- The hardware: most Cedar machines were Dorados, a very powerful machine where a simple Algol-style call and return takes under 10 microseconds, with a 24-bit virtual address space (of 16-bit words) and an 80-megabyte disk; the authors liken a Dorado to an IBM 370/168 dedicated to one user. (p.41)
- Communication is typically over a 3-megabit-per-second Ethernet (some on 10 Mbit); the research internetwork has about 160 Ethernets connected by leased telephone and satellite links at 4800-56000 bps. Most traffic is on the local Ethernet; offered loads above 40 percent of Ethernet capacity are very rare, 10 percent typical. (p.41)
- The PUP family of protocols provides uniform internetwork access, including an unreliable datagram service and reliable flow-controlled byte streams; raw Ethernet packets are available between two machines on the same Ethernet. (p.41)
- Essentially all programming is in high-level languages; the dominant one is Mesa (as modified for Cedar), with Smalltalk and InterLisp also used, and no assembly language for Dorados. (p.41)
- The minimum average round-trip time for a packet exchange on the 10 Mbit Ethernet is 120 microseconds. (p.42-43)

## Section 1.3: Aims
- Primary purpose: make distributed computation easy. The authors observed that building communicating programs was previously a task only for a select group of communication experts; the existing communication mechanisms were a major factor constraining distributed computing. (p.41)
- The hope: provide communication with almost as much ease as local procedure calls, leaving only the fundamental difficulties of distributed systems: timing, independent failure of components, coexistence of independent execution environments. (p.41)
- Secondary aim 1 (efficiency): keep RPC within about a factor of five of the network's necessary transmission times, lest application designers avoid communication and distort their designs. (p.41-42)
- Secondary aim 2 (powerful semantics without losing simplicity or efficiency); the design tension is between powerful semantics and efficiency. (p.42)
- Final major aim (security): provide secure communication, because no previously implemented protocol protected data in transit (even passwords were sent as clear-text); secure end-to-end RPC had apparently never been built. (p.42)

## Section 1.4: Fundamental decisions
- Choosing procedure calls over message passing was not forced by the aims; the authors judged the two would not differ much in the problems faced, and chose procedure calls because that was the main control-and-data-transfer mechanism in Mesa. (p.42)
- They also considered and rejected a remote-fork (more parallel) paradigm, again because it would not change the major design problems. (p.42)
- They discarded emulating a shared address space among machines: possible difficulties were integrating remote addresses into the languages (and perhaps machine architecture) and achieving acceptable efficiency; a naive implementation would widen language addresses by the 16-bit PUP host address. They judged the cost would exceed the benefit on their hardware and assumed the absence of shared addresses. (p.42-43) [Partly stated in the note's Birrell & Nelson callout in "Pointers in RPC": discarding shared-address-space emulation as too expensive for their hardware; the specific difficulties and the 16-bit PUP host-address widening remain beyond the notes.]
- Guiding principle: remote-call semantics should be as close as possible to local-call semantics, to keep the facility easy to use for programmers familiar with single-machine use. (p.43) [The principle is now stated in the note's Birrell & Nelson callout in "RPC Requirements"; the ease-of-use rationale for programmers familiar with single-machine use remains beyond the notes.]
- A consequence of that principle, deviating from common practice: no time-out limits a call's duration in the absence of failure, because local calls have no time-out and the languages already include mechanisms to abort an activity. (p.43) [Now stated in the note's Birrell & Nelson callout in "RPC Requirements" (no time-out because local calls have none), except that the languages already include mechanisms to abort an activity, which remains beyond the notes.]
- Another consequence: the authors chose binding semantics based closely on the existing Cedar mechanisms (the scheme of Section 2) in preference to those in Nelson's thesis. The paper text literally reads "building semantics described below"; the forward reference is to the binding section. (p.43)

## Section 1.5: Structure (the five pieces)
- Five pieces of program are involved in a remote call: the user, the user-stub, the RPCRuntime (the RPC communications package), the server-stub, and the server. The user, user-stub, and one RPCRuntime run on the caller machine; the server, server-stub, and another RPCRuntime on the callee machine. (p.43) [Now stated in the note's Birrell & Nelson callout in "Structure of RPC", so not beyond the notes.]
- The user makes an ordinary local call into the user-stub; the user-stub packs a specification of the target procedure and the arguments into one or more packets and asks RPCRuntime to transmit them reliably; the callee RPCRuntime passes them to the server-stub, which unpacks and makes an ordinary local call into the server. The caller's process is suspended awaiting a result packet. (p.43) [Now stated in the note body (remote-procedure-calls.md, sections Structure of RPC and Steps in RPC), with the paper's piece names in the note's Birrell & Nelson callout, so not beyond the notes.]
- RPCRuntime is responsible for retransmissions, acknowledgments, packet routing, and encryption. The claim: if user and server were brought into one machine and bound directly without the stubs, the program would still work. (p.43-44) [The stubless direct-linking claim is now stated in the note's Birrell & Nelson callout in "Structure of RPC"; the RPCRuntime's responsibilities remain beyond the notes.]
- RPCRuntime is a standard part of Cedar. The stubs are generated automatically by a program called *Lupine* from Mesa interface modules; these interface modules are the basis of Mesa/Cedar separate compilation and binding, and consist mainly of a list of procedure names with argument and result types. (p.44) [Lupine generating the stubs from Mesa interface modules is now stated in the note's Birrell & Nelson callout in "Structure of RPC"; the RPCRuntime being a standard part of Cedar and the description of interface modules remain beyond the notes.]
- Terminology: a module implementing an interface's procedures *exports* it; a module calling them *imports* it. A programmer writes an interface module, then the importing user code and the exporting server code, and presents the interface to Lupine, which generates the user-stub (exporting the interface) and the server-stub (importing it). (p.44)
- Lupine generates the packing/unpacking code and the dispatch to the correct procedure for an incoming call; the programmer must avoid arguments or results incompatible with the lack of a shared address space, and Lupine checks this; the programmer must invoke the binding and handle reported failures. (p.44) [The programmer's avoidance of address-incompatible arguments, checked by Lupine, is now stated in the note's Birrell & Nelson callout in "Pointers in RPC"; the generated dispatch code and the programmer invoking binding and handling failures remain beyond the notes.]

## Section 2.1-2.2: Binding, naming, and locating an exporter
- There are two aspects to binding: how a client specifies what it wants to bind to (naming), and how a caller finds the callee's machine address and specifies the procedure (location). (p.44-45)
- An interface name has two parts: the *type* (which interface the caller expects, at some level of abstraction, e.g. the abstraction "mail server") and the *instance* (which particular implementor, e.g. one particular mail server). A reasonable default type name is derived from the Mesa interface module name. The semantics of an interface name are an agreement between exporter and importer, not fully enforced by the RPC package. (p.45) [The type/instance split is now stated in the note's Birrell & Nelson callout in "Binding and Registry"; the mail-server example, the default type name, and the exporter-importer agreement remain beyond the notes.]
- Binding uses the *Grapevine* distributed database; its attraction is being widely and reliably available, distributed across multiple servers and keeping at least three copies of each entry, so lookups almost never fail. The alternatives (hard-coding network addresses in applications, or broadcast) were judged unsatisfactory. (p.45) [Grapevine as a distributed database replicated across several servers is now stated in the note's Birrell & Nelson callout in "Binding and Registry"; the three copies per entry and the rejected alternatives remain beyond the notes.]
- Grapevine entries are keyed by a character-string RName and come in two kinds: *individuals* (with a *connect-site*, a network address) and *groups* (with a *member-list* of RNames). The RPC package keeps two entries per interface name: the instance is a Grapevine individual whose connect-site is the exporting machine's address; the type is a Grapevine group whose members are the instances of that type. (p.45-46)
- Worked naming example: type FileAccess.Alpine with instance Ebbets.Alpine exported from address 3#22#, and instance Luther.Alpine from 3#276#; the group FileAccess.Alpine lists both; the individual Ebbets.Alpine has connect-site 3#22#. (p.46)
- Export procedure: the server-stub calls `ExportInterface` in RPCRuntime with the interface name and a *dispatcher* procedure; ExportInterface updates Grapevine so the instance is in the type group and has the right connect-site. As an optimization, the database is not updated if already correct, which is usually the case. (p.46)
- The exporting machine's RPCRuntime records, per exported interface, a table holding the interface name, the dispatcher, and a 32-bit permanently-unique machine-relative identifier of the export; the table is an array indexed by a small integer. The identifier is kept unique by successive values of a 32-bit counter initialized on start-up to a one-second real-time clock, which constrains the average rate of `ExportInterface` calls to under one per second since restart (burst rate can exceed this). (p.46)
- Import procedure: the user-stub calls `ImportInterface` with the desired type and instance; RPCRuntime asks Grapevine for the connect-site, then makes a remote call to that machine's RPCRuntime for the binding information; if the machine is not exporting the interface, binding fails; otherwise its table yields the unique identifier and table index, which are returned and remembered by the user-stub. (p.46-47)
- On a subsequent call, the user-stub's call packet carries the unique identifier, the table index, and the entry-point number of the procedure; the callee's RPCRuntime looks up the table by index, verifies the unique identifier matches, and passes the packet to the dispatcher. (p.47)
- Binding variants: specifying only the type (no instance) makes RPCRuntime enumerate the group members and try their addresses "in an order which tends to locate the closest (most responsive) running exporter", binding to the closest instance of a replicated service; or the instance may be a network-address constant, binding without any Grapevine interaction. (p.47-48) [Binding to the closest running instance when only the type is given is now stated in the note's Birrell & Nelson callout in "Binding and Registry"; the group-enumeration mechanism and the network-address-constant variant remain beyond the notes.]

## Section 2.3: Discussion of the binding scheme
- Importing an interface has no effect on the exporting machine's data structures, which is advantageous for servers with hundreds of users and avoids questions about what the server should do on importer crashes. (p.48) [Now stated in the note's Birrell & Nelson callout in "Binding and Registry", except the avoided question of what the server should do on importer crashes, which remains beyond the notes.]
- Bindings are implicitly broken if the exporter crashes and restarts, because the unique identifier's currency is checked on every call; the authors argue this implicit unbinding is correct, since otherwise a user would not be notified of a between-calls crash. (p.48) [The implicit break, via the unique identifier checked on every call, is now stated in the note's Birrell & Nelson callout in "Binding and Registry"; the authors' argument that this is correct remains beyond the notes.]
- The scheme allows calls only on explicitly exported procedures; a more efficient alternative (handing importers the exporter's internal dispatcher representation) was rejected because it would allow unchecked access to almost any procedure and defeat protection/security. (p.48)
- Grapevine's access controls restrict who can export particular interface names, which the authors call the desired semantics (a random user should not be able to claim to be a mail server and intercept traffic); for replicated services this access control is critical to secure two-way authentication. (p.48)
- Binding-time choices, from most to least flexible: type only (instance decided dynamically); instance as an RName (most common, delaying the choice of machine); instance as a network address (binding to a machine at compile time). There are also facilities to dynamically instantiate and import interfaces, letting an importer bind to several exporting machines without knowing statically how many, which was useful in open-ended algorithms such as a distributed-transaction manager. (p.48-49)
- Binding is not allowed at a finer grain than an entire interface; finer-grained binding was not considered useful in the systems observed. (p.49)

## Section 3.1: Packet-level transport protocol requirements
- RPC semantics could be achieved without a specialized packet protocol (e.g. over PUP byte streams, as the NS "Courier" RPC protocol uses the NS sequenced packet protocol), but the authors' measurements and experience convinced them this was unsatisfactory: a transport protocol designed specially for RPC could gain a factor of ten in performance. (p.49-50)
- Design emphases: minimize elapsed real-time between initiating a call and getting results (unlike bulk-transfer protocols where transfer time dominates), and minimize the load a server bears from many clients, so expensive connection setup/teardown and large per-connection state are unacceptable. (p.50)
- The guarantee the protocol level defines: if the call returns to the user, the server procedure was invoked *exactly once*; otherwise an exception is reported and the procedure was invoked *either once or not at all*, and the user is not told which, nor whether the fault was a server crash or a communication problem. Provided the server RPCRuntime still responds, there is no upper bound on how long results are awaited; a call is aborted on communication breakdown or crash but not if the server code deadlocks or loops, identical to local-call semantics. (p.49-50) [The exactly-once / once-or-not-at-all guarantee, without telling the user which or why, is now stated in the note's Birrell & Nelson callout in "Handling Partial Failures"; the unbounded wait and the no-abort-on-deadlock behavior remain beyond the notes.]

## Section 3.2: Simple calls
- The common case optimized for: all arguments fit in one packet, all results fit in one packet, and calls are frequent. To call, the caller sends a call packet with a call identifier, data specifying the procedure, and the arguments; the callee invokes the procedure and sends a result packet with the same call identifier and the results. (p.50)
- The transmitter is responsible for retransmitting until acknowledged, but the result of a call acknowledges the call packet and the next call packet acknowledges the previous result packet; so when call duration and inter-call interval are each less than the retransmission interval, exactly two packets are sent per call (one each way). Longer calls or gaps add up to two more packets (a retransmission and an explicit acknowledgment). (p.50)
- The *call identifier* lets the caller confirm the result is for its current call (not a delayed earlier result) and lets the callee eliminate duplicate call packets. It consists of the (permanent, globally unique) calling machine identifier, a machine-relative calling-process identifier, and a sequence number. The pair [machine, process] is an *activity*. (p.50) [Call identifiers and a per-activity sequence number letting the callee discard duplicates are now stated in the note's Birrell & Nelson callout in "What is SunRPC?"; the caller-side check, the identifier's parts, and the activity definition remain beyond the notes.]
- Key property: each activity has at most one outstanding remote call at a time (it will not start a new call until it receives the previous result). The sequence number must be monotonic per activity (not necessarily sequential). The callee RPCRuntime keeps a table of the last sequence number per calling activity; a call packet whose sequence number is not greater than the table value is discarded as a duplicate (possibly after acknowledgment). (p.50)
- Connection management compared to heavyweight protocols: a "connection" is the shared state between a calling activity and the server machine's RPCRuntime; no connection-establishment handshake is needed (receipt of a call packet from a new activity creates the connection implicitly); an idle connection costs only a sequence-number table entry at the server and a single machine-wide counter at the caller; no "pinging" maintains idle connections; there is no explicit termination (the server may discard idle state after, say, five minutes, without interacting with the caller). (p.50-51)
- The duplicate-elimination relies on the per-export unique identifier (to handle a server crashing and restarting while a caller retransmits) and on a 32-bit *conversation identifier* (used for secure calls) that distinguishes incarnations of a calling machine, so a restarted machine's sequence numbers are not mistakenly treated as duplicates; conversation identifiers are generated from a 32-bit clock initialized from network time servers at restart. (p.51)

## Section 3.3: Complicated calls
- When retransmitting, the packet is modified to request an explicit acknowledgment; this handles lost packets, long-duration calls, and long gaps. While awaiting a result the caller periodically sends a *probe* packet the callee must acknowledge, so the caller notices a crashed callee or serious communication failure; as long as probes are acknowledged the caller waits indefinitely. (p.52)
- Probe timing: the first probe is sent after slightly more than the approximate round-trip time; the interval grows gradually until, after about 10 minutes, probes are sent every five minutes; each probe is retransmitted like other packets. This detects only communication-level failures, not a callee deadlocked on the call, in keeping with the local-call-semantics principle. (p.52)
- The authors considered and rejected an alternative (the recipient spontaneously generating an acknowledgment when it will not produce the next packet in time), judging the saved packet not worth the extra server-side data structure and process cost. (p.52)
- If arguments or results exceed one packet, they are sent in multiple packets, each but the last requesting an explicit acknowledgment; the caller and callee alternate (data packet, acknowledgment), which lets the implementation use only one packet buffer per end and avoids bulk-transfer buffering/flow control. Each data packet within a call has a call-relative sequence number for duplicate elimination. (p.52)
- The protocol admits it sends more packets than logically required for multi-packet calls (up to twice as many as a good bulk-data protocol, since each packet is acknowledged), which is poor across long-haul networks with large delays; the authors keep RPC and bulk data as separate protocols, note RPC can carry bulk data by multiplexing single-packet calls across processes, and leave unexplored the option of switching to a conventional protocol for complicated calls. (p.52-54)

## Section 3.4: Exception handling
- Mesa provides elaborate exception facilities (called *signals*), thought of as dynamically bound procedure activations: raising a signal makes the runtime scan the call stack for a catch phrase, which may return (resuming where the exception was raised) or jump out (unwinding newer activations most-recent-first). (p.54)
- The RPC package emulates this across machines: the server can send an exception packet in place of a result packet; the caller's RPCRuntime handles it like a call packet but raises the exception in the appropriate process; a catch phrase that returns sends results back to the callee; one that jumps notifies the callee to unwind. The callee may communicate only exceptions declared in the exported Mesa interface, which simplifies name translation and aids protection and debugging. (p.54)
- In addition to callee-raised exceptions, RPCRuntime may raise a *call failed* exception on communication difficulty; this is the primary way clients tell remote from local calls. (p.54) ["Call failed" being the primary way clients tell remote from local calls is now stated in the note's Birrell & Nelson callout in "Handling Partial Failures"; RPCRuntime raising it on communication difficulty, alongside callee-raised exceptions, remains beyond the notes.]

## Section 3.5: Use of processes
- In Mesa/Cedar, parallel processes are a built-in feature: forking costs about as much as ten local procedure calls, and a process swap swaps an evaluation stack and one register plus invalidating cached information; still, on the scale of a remote call these costs matter, so the authors kept them low. (p.54)
- Each machine keeps a stock of idle server processes to avoid process-creation cost per call; a finished server process reverts to idle rather than dying, and excess idle processes created during a peak kill themselves. (p.55)
- Each packet carries source and destination process identifiers; an interrupt handler dispatches an incoming packet directly to a waiting process if the destination process is waiting, otherwise to an idle server process; a new call reuses the identifier of the process that handled the activity's previous call (likely still waiting to acknowledge). (p.55)
- Result: simple calls create no processes and involve typically four process swaps (the theoretical minimum without busy-waiting is two; the extra two arise because an interrupt handler, not device microcode, dispatches incoming packets). (p.55-56)

## Section 3.6: Other optimizations
- Summary of adopted optimizations: implicit acknowledgment via subsequent packets, minimizing connection-maintenance cost, avoiding connection setup/teardown, reducing process switches. (p.56)
- A further optimization: when caller and callee are on the same network, RPC packets bypass the normal protocol-hierarchy software layers (the network driver treats RPC packets as a special case); the internetwork protocol hierarchy is still used for internetwork routing. The authors call this "cheating" that works only because RPC is the special case, but argue it is a general truth that a particular transport protocol can gain significantly by bypassing lower-layer generality. (p.56)
- Reasonable optimizations deliberately not used: avoiding the internet packet format on the local network, specialized packet formats for simple calls, special-purpose network microcode, forbidding non-RPC communication, busy-waits instead of process swaps. Using them might have given another factor of two in performance. (p.56)

## Section 3.7: Security
- The package includes encryption-based security using Grapevine as the authentication service / key distribution center and the federal Data Encryption Standard; callers and callees are mutually assured of identity; full end-to-end encryption protects against eavesdropping and detects modification, replay, or creation of calls. The details are deferred to a later paper. (p.56)

## Section 4: Performance
- Measurements were made between two Dorados on a lightly loaded 2.94-megabit Ethernet, running Cedar, counting Dorado microprocessor cycles and dividing by the crystal frequency, accurate to within about ten percent; times are elapsed times for 12,000 calls per procedure. The interval measured is from the user's invocation of the user-stub procedure through its return, covering both stubs, both RPCRuntimes, the server implementation, and transmission both ways; no encryption was used. (p.56-57)
- Table I results (microseconds, minimum and median, with calculated transmission time and purely-local-call time), the figures the lecture never gives:
  - no args/results: 1059 min, 1097 median, 131 transmission, 9 local-only.
  - 1 arg/result: 1070 / 1105 / 142 / 10.
  - 2 args/results: 1077 / 1127 / 152 / 11.
  - 4 args/results: 1115 / 1171 / 174 / 12.
  - 10 args/results: 1222 / 1278 / 239 / 17.
  - 1-word array: 1069 / 1111 / 131 / 10.
  - 4-word array: 1106 / 1153 / 174 / 13.
  - 10-word array: 1214 / 1250 / 239 / 16.
  - 40-word array: 1643 / 1695 / 566 / 51.
  - 100-word array: 2915 / 2926 / 1219 / 98.
  - resume exception: 2555 / 2637 / 284 / 134.
  - unwind exception: 3374 / 3467 / 284 / 196. (p.57)
- Each of the first five procedures had 0, 1, 2, 4, 10 arguments and the same number of results, each 16 bits; the next five had one array argument/result of 1, 4, 10, 40, 100 words; the last two raise an exception the caller resumes, and one the caller unwinds. (p.57)
- By interleaving parallel calls from multiple processes, the authors reached 2 megabits/second transferring between Dorado main memories on the 3 Mbit Ethernet, equal to their best byte-stream implementation (written in BCPL). (p.57-58)
- They did not measure export/import cost, which is dominated by talking to Grapevine; after locating the exporter, getting the dispatcher identifier uses one RPC with a few words of data. (p.58)

## Section 5: Status and discussion
- The package is fully implemented and in use by Cedar programmers; the whole RPCRuntime is four Cedar modules (packet exchange, packet sequencing, binding, security) totalling about 2,200 lines; Lupine is substantially larger. (p.58)
- Clients used RPC for Alpine (a file server supporting multimachine transactions), the control communication for an Ethernet telephone/audio project, and two network games with real-time communication. Protocol implementations exist for BCPL, InterLisp, SmallTalk, and C. (p.58)
- The authors note situations where RPC seems the wrong paradigm, corresponding to cases where multicasting or broadcasting is more appropriate, and raise the open question of whether a general-purpose transport protocol could reach RPC-level performance (they had not seen it achieved). (p.58)
- They judge their contributions of general interest: a particular point in the RPC design spectrum, good performance without extreme measures, transport-connection management that minimizes cost and server state, and powerful-yet-simple binding semantics that were easy and efficient to implement. (p.58-59)

## Notes vs paper discrepancies
Each item was checked against the paper text and at least one third-party source; sources are listed under "Discrepancy sources". Verdicts: LECTURE FRAMING (the note reproduces the lecture and the paper differs; keep the lecture version, annotate with a callout); NOTES WRONG (the note departs from both paper and lecture; fixed in place); NOT A REAL DISCREPANCY.

- The note presents binding as a choice between a distributed registry and a per-machine registry, and treats the per-machine registry as one extreme. The paper's actual binding uses Grapevine, a replicated distributed database, binding importer-to-exporter by interface type and instance, with implicit unbinding on exporter restart. Annotated with a callout after the registry-naming paragraph.
  - Verdict: LECTURE FRAMING. The lecture's registry taxonomy is a teaching generalization; the per-machine registry is in fact SunRPC's portmapper, not this paper's design. Third parties (Tanenbaum & Van Steen, *Distributed Systems*; the ONC RPC binding RFC 1833) confirm the per-machine binder is Sun's choice, while Birrell & Nelson's own Section 2.2 describes the Grapevine scheme. Both are kept: the lecture framing in prose, the paper's scheme in the callout.
- The note says RPC "handles a lot of the errors" and provides a catch-all failure notification, without stating any call-semantics guarantee. The paper is precise: return implies exactly-once, an exception implies once-or-not-at-all. Added as a callout, not a correction.

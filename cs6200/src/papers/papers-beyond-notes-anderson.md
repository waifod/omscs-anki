The paper is Thomas E. Anderson, "The Performance of Spin Lock Alternatives for Shared-Memory Multiprocessors", IEEE Transactions on Parallel and Distributed Systems, vol. 1, no. 1, January 1990, pp. 6-16. Six numbered sections plus references. Citations below give the section (and figure/table where relevant); the extraction has printed page numbers 6-16. ## Abstract and index terms
- The abstract states the core question directly: given hardware support for atomic instructions, are there efficient software spin-waiting algorithms, or is more complex hardware support needed for performance? (abstract)
- The abstract frames the method as an analogy: arbitration for control of a lock is in many ways similar to arbitration for control of a network connecting a distributed system, so the paper applies static and dynamic network-arbitration methods to spin locks. (abstract)
- The abstract previews all three contributions: the CSMA-derived delay methods, the novel software queueing method (unique sequence number per arriving processor), and several hardware solutions that reduce the cost of spin-waiting. (abstract)
- Index terms: Architecture, cache coherence, locking, multiprocessor, performance. (front matter)
- The paper was received April 21, 1989, revised August 25, 1989; the author was with the Department of Computer Science and Engineering, University of Washington, Seattle. The work was supported by NSF grants and by, among others, the Washington Technology Center and Digital Equipment Corporation (SRC and External Research Program). (manuscript footnote)

## Section I: Introduction
- Named real machines of the era, none in the notes: commercially successful shared-memory multiprocessors are the Sequent Symmetry, Alliant FX, and BBN Butterfly; research vehicles include the DEC SRC Firefly, Illinois Cedar, IBM RP3, and Wisconsin Multicube. (Section I)
- Pure software mutual exclusion is expensive (cites Lamport's fast mutual exclusion algorithm), which is why virtually all shared-memory multiprocessors provide hardware atomic instructions. (Section I)
- Atomic instructions serve two distinct purposes: (1) if the operation on shared data is simple enough it can be encapsulated in a single atomic instruction and mutual exclusion is then guaranteed directly in hardware; (2) for critical sections longer than one instruction, atomics arbitrate acquisition of a lock while waiting is done in software. The notes only use the second. (Section I)
- Herlihy is cited on the computational power of atomic instructions for building parallel algorithms. (Section I)
- The paper is explicit about when spin-waiting (vs blocking) is the right choice: it is useful in exactly two situations, when the critical section is small (so the expected wait is less than the cost of blocking and resuming) or when there is no other work for the processor to do. The notes present spinlocks without this cost comparison. (Section I)
- Jayasimha, and Agarwal and Cherian, are cited as having looked at the related problem of efficient spin-waiting for data dependencies (as opposed to locks). (Section I)
- A thesis the notes do not state: it might seem that contention performance does not matter, because a highly parallel application by definition has no heavily contended lock, and a bottleneck lock should be removed by redesigning the algorithm rather than by adding processors. (Section I)
- The paper then rebuts its own thesis with four situations where contention performance does matter: (1) a heavily utilized lock prevents an application reaching peak performance because the average number of spinners grows as the lock approaches saturation; (2) bursty arrivals cause temporarily long queues and bad short-term performance even without a long-term bottleneck; (3) an operating system cannot control the rate at which users make system calls, so normally quiet locks can become contended at high load; (4) on a multiprogrammed multiprocessor a naive user can ruin performance for everyone by combining a bottleneck critical section, many processors, and an inefficient spin lock. (Section I)
- The three design concerns are stated as a tradeoff the notes present only as the three metrics: minimizing the communication bandwidth consumed by spinners, minimizing the delay between release and re-acquisition, and keeping latency (acquisition cost under no contention) low; "the more frequently a processor tries to acquire a lock, the faster it will be acquired, but the more other processors will be disrupted." (Section I) [The three concerns themselves are now stated in the note body (synchronization-constructs.md, section Spinlock Performance Metrics); the quoted tradeoff is not.]

## Section II: Range of multiprocessor architectures considered
- The paper studies six specific architecture types, which the notes compress into "interconnect vs bus" and "write-update vs write-invalidate": (1) multistage interconnection network without coherent caches; (2) multistage network with invalidation-based coherence using remote directories; (3) bus without coherent caches; (4) bus with snoopy write-through invalidation; (5) bus with snoopy write-back invalidation; (6) bus with snoopy distributed-write coherence. (Section II)
- A simplifying assumption stated once and used throughout: processors block when making a read request to memory. (Section II)
- "Distributed-write" is the paper's term for what the notes call write-update. The paper uses distributed-write (and "write-broadcast") throughout; the notes use write-update. (Section II, III)

### II.A: Common hardware support for mutual exclusion
- An atomic read-modify-write conceptually requires four services that may need interprocessor communication: the read, the write, arbitration between simultaneous requests, and some state that blocks further accesses while the instruction executes. Most multiprocessors collapse these into one or two bus or network transactions. The notes do not decompose the atomic this way. (Section II.A)
- Multistage-network detail: when an atomic reads a location, any cached copies recorded in the memory module's directory must be invalidated and further accesses delayed (or refused and retried) while the new value is computed; to cut this delay the computation can be done remotely by an ALU attached to each memory module. The Butterfly and RP3 implement this "fetch and op". (Section II.A)
- Single-bus detail: a processor acquires the bus and raises an "atomic bus line" before starting an atomic; the line is held while the value is computed to block other atomics, but the bus itself can be released to let normal memory requests proceed, and waiting atomics only re-arbitrate when the line drops. The notes' "atomics go to the memory controller" hides this bus-line mechanism. (Section II.A)
- Key complication for the lecture's "atomics bypass the cache": with invalidation-based coherence the atomic-bus-line acquisition can be overlapped with the invalidation signal, and the invalidation happens before the instruction executes even if the value does not change. Write-back invalidation avoids an extra write transaction by taking the dirty value from the holding cache at the moment it invalidates it. With distributed-write the initial read is usually unnecessary because the block is already cached, so piggy-backing the atomic-bus-line arbitration on top of bus arbitration would waste bus cycles, and the Firefly therefore has a separate arbitration mechanism for its atomic bus line; a bus cycle is still usually needed at the end to update other caches. (Section II.A) [Partly annotated in the note with a Paper callout: the write-back dirty-value pickup, the distributed-write skipped read and final bus cycle, and invalidation before execution even on an unchanged value are stated there; the overlap with the invalidation signal and the Firefly's separate arbitration mechanism are not.]

## Section III: The performance of simple approaches to spin-waiting
- A correct simple spin lock is "relatively straightforward": each processor test-and-sets to acquire (the instruction reads the old value and sets it busy); release clears the value atomically. The difficulty is efficiency, not correctness. (Section III)

### III.A: Spin on test-and-set
- Table I gives the code: `Init lock := CLEAR; Lock: while (TestAndSet(lock) = BUSY); Unlock: lock := CLEAR`. (Section III.A, Table I)
- Two named causes of degradation, the first of which the notes omit: (1) to release the lock, the holder must contend with spinning processors for exclusive access to the lock location, and most architectures cannot give priority to the holder's clear request, so the release waits behind doomed test-and-sets; (2) on shared bus/network, spinners' requests slow all other memory traffic, which on multistage networks causes a "hot-spot" (cites Pfister and Norton) and on buses can saturate the bus since each test-and-set is at least one bus transaction regardless of whether it changes the value. (Section III.A)

### III.B: Spin on read (test-and-test-and-set)
- The idea is attributed to Segall and Rudolph, not to Anderson. Table II: `while (lock = BUSY or TestAndSet(lock) = BUSY)`. The paper assumes Boolean short-circuit evaluation, so the test-and-set runs only if the lock is not busy. The notes present the construct without attribution. (Section III.B, Table II) [Attribution and the short-circuit assumption are now stated in the note's Anderson callout, and the predicate is in the note body (section Test and Test and Set Spinlock), so not beyond the notes.]
- The central transient argument the notes miss: when the critical section is small, spin-on-read "has almost as much effect on busy processors as spinning directly on a test-and-set instruction", because transient behaviour dominates. When the lock is released and reacquired it takes time for the remaining processors to resume looping in their caches, and during that interim most spinners have pending memory requests that delay busy processors. (Section III.B) [Annotated in the note.]
- The step-by-step cascade: on release all cache copies are invalidated; each processor read-misses to refetch, and these misses are satisfied serially; each processor that gets the value tries a test-and-set; the first succeeds, but any processor that completed its read miss before that will have seen the lock free, do a doomed test-and-set, fail, and resume spinning; each failing test-and-set, being a write, re-invalidates all copies and forces those processors to miss again. The system quiesces only when the last spinner finally test-and-sets. (Section III.B) [Now stated in the note body (section Test and Test and Set Spinlock Quiz, write-invalidate answer) and the note's spin-on-read Anderson callout, except that the read misses are satisfied serially and that quiescence comes only when the last spinner test-and-sets.]
- A fixed-priority-arbitration subtlety (e.g. the Firefly): spinning on a cache copy might seem to protect the high-priority lock holder, but if the lock is released before quiescence, a low-priority processor with a pending test-and-set can grab it ahead of higher-priority processors still looping on read, and the holder can then be delayed by those higher-priority processors. (Section III.B)

### III.C: Reasons for the poor performance of spin on read
- Three distinct root causes, which the notes collapse into "write-invalidate is bad": (1) the separation between detecting the release and committing to the test-and-set lets more than one processor pass the test; (2) a test-and-set invalidates cached copies even when it does not change the value; (3) invalidation-based coherence needs O(P) bus/network cycles to broadcast one value to P waiting processors, even though they all then request exactly the same data. (Section III.C) [Now stated in the note's Anderson callout (spin-on-read release), so not beyond the notes.]
- Fixing any one cause alone still leaves bus activity growing linearly with the number of processors. This is the paper's basis for the lecture's O(N^2) claim: the per-release and per-invalidation linear factors compound. (Section III.C) [Annotated in the note.]
- Distributed-write worked example: it eliminates invalidations (each processor receives updates directly, all reads are local, only test-and-sets need the bus), and the Sequent Balance and Silicon Graphics 4D-MP each use a separate distributed-write bus just for test-and-set variables. But distributed-write makes the test/test-and-set separation worse, because all processors receive the released value simultaneously and all rush to test-and-set, so P test-and-sets occur before quiescence. (Section III.C) [The rush to test-and-set (P test-and-sets on release) is stated in the note body (section Test and Test and Set Spinlock Quiz, write-update answer); the elimination of invalidations and the Balance / 4D-MP separate bus are not.]

### III.D: Measurement results (simple approaches)
- The measurement platform, which the notes give only partially: a Sequent Symmetry Model B with twenty 80386 processors (about 2 MIP each), shared bus, write-back invalidation-based coherence; unlike the Balance, test-and-set variables share the normal memory bus. Acquiring and releasing a lock normally takes 5.6 microseconds (less if the lock's cache block is already private to the locker). (Section III.D) [The note body states Sequent Symmetry, twenty processors, write-invalidate (section Spinlock Performance Comparisons); the rest is not in the notes.]
- Benchmark construction (the same benchmark underlies Fig. 1 and the lecture's figure, which is the paper's Fig. 3): each processor loops one million / P times doing acquire, critical section, release, and a random delay drawn from a uniform distribution with mean five times the critical-section size. The random wait deliberately eliminates locality so the lock and shared data move between caches each iteration; the lock and shared data are placed in separate cache blocks. (Section III.D, Fig. 1)
- The "ideal" curve is defined precisely: the time the test would take given free spin-waiting, determined by simulation from the critical-section time and the mean inter-access delay. (Section III.D)
- Fig. 1 shows the actual elapsed time (not just overhead), with spin-on-test-and-set, spin-on-read, and ideal. Spin-on-test-and-set degrades badly; spin-on-read is better but still disappointing; peak ideal performance is never reached; performance is very sensitive to adding even a few processors past saturation. (Section III.D, Fig. 1)
- A degenerative-feedback observation the notes omit: critical sections have higher memory-access rates than non-critical code (their purpose is to touch shared structures), so as a lock becomes a bottleneck the spinners slow the holder both absolutely and relatively, making it more of a bottleneck and spawning yet more spinners. (Section III.D)
- Fig. 2 and the quiesce experiment: Anderson measures time-to-quiesce by building a critical section that first delays without using the bus and then uses the bus heavily; varying the initial delay finds a sharp "knee". The time to quiesce grows steeply but linearly with the number of spinning processors, so even a few spinners hurt a moderate critical section. The notes do not mention Fig. 2 or the quiesce measurement at all. (Section III.D, Fig. 2) [The linear growth of time-to-quiesce with spinners (Fig. 2) is now stated in the note's callout; the knee experiment method is not.]

## Section IV: New software alternatives
- The section describes five software approaches: four delay-based (from CSMA network protocols) plus explicit queueing. (Section IV)

### IV.A: Delay alternatives
- The four delay variants are organized along two dimensions, which the notes present less systematically: where the delay goes (after the lock is released, Table III; or after every reference, Table IV) crossed with how the delay is set (static or dynamic). Because a processor tries to acquire the lock before delaying, lock latency is unaffected. (Section IV.A, Tables III-IV)
- The CSMA lineage is explicit, which the notes omit: statically assigned slots resemble BRAM (Chlamtac et al.); static delays resemble Aloha; dynamic backoff is Ethernet's exponential backoff (Metcalfe and Boggs). Slots can also implement priority access to the critical section by assigning lower delays to higher-priority processors. (Section IV.A) [The BRAM / Aloha / Ethernet lineage is now stated in the note's callout; the priority-access use of slots is not.]
- Static-slot mechanics: each processor gets a slot 0..P-1; the smallest-delay spinner reacquires, the rest time out, see the lock busy (another cache miss), and resume. Static slots guarantee at most one processor times out at an instant. The number of slots trades the two regimes: fewer slots is better with few spinners (faster handoff), more slots is better with many spinners (fewer simultaneous timeouts). (Section IV.A)
- Exponential-backoff mechanics the notes do not give: a processor estimates the number of spinners from the number of collisions it experiences; a collision is unlikely once the mean delay is at least half the number of spinners, so Ethernet doubles the mean delay after each collision. Translated to locks, a processor doubles its mean delay each time it times out, sees the lock free, test-and-sets, and fails. (Section IV.A) [The doubling on each collision is now stated in the note's callout; estimating spinners from collisions and the half-the-spinners threshold are not.]
- The network analogy is explicitly imperfect, a caveat the notes drop: in a network a collision aborts all parties and costs the same regardless of how many collide, whereas a test-and-set collision still lets one processor win and its cost rises with the number colliding (more collisions means longer to quiesce and more slowing of the holder). (Section IV.A) [Annotated in the note.]
- Three backoff design details Anderson's first implementation got wrong: (1) do not change the mean delay merely because some other processor had a shorter delay (seeing the lock acquired says little about the number of spinners); (2) bound the mean delay, and set the bound equal to the number of processors so backoff matches static slots under heavy contention; (3) start each visit at a fraction (he used half) of the previous delay rather than from zero, because the learning curve is expensive for locks. (Section IV.A) [Details (2) and (3), the bound equal to the number of processors and starting at half the previous delay, are now stated in the note's callout; detail (1) is not.]
- Backoff with non-random arrivals: if processors run for a fixed time between accesses they self-schedule into either no contention or a steady number of spinners; backoff raises delays until collisions are rare and hysteresis maintains them. Both backoff and static slots degrade on repeated bursty arrivals (all choose small delays, collide, take long to quiesce), and both end a burst with inappropriately long delays. (Section IV.A)
- Applicability limits the notes gloss over: polling for release is only practical with per-processor coherent caches; on other systems spinners consume bandwidth, and some remote-directory multistage machines cap the number of outstanding copies of a location, so if spinners exceed that cap spin-on-read "degenerates to spinning across the network". Even with snoopy/full-directory invalidation, backoff or static slots fix only one of the spin-on-read problems: each spinner still needs at least two read misses per critical section (one on release, one on acquire), which can saturate the bus. (Section IV.A)
- Backoff-after-release is scalable specifically on distributed-write machines, where each test-and-set is one broadcast bus cycle independent of P and backoff limits the number of failing test-and-sets. (Section IV.A)
- Delay-between-each-reference specifics: usable on non-coherent or invalidation-based machines to cap bandwidth; it checks the lock before test-and-set because a test-and-set costs more bandwidth than a read; but backoff here behaves badly for a single spinner on a moderate critical section, because the processor keeps backing off while the lock is held and is then mid-delay when the lock is finally released, so it is slow to notice. (Section IV.A)

### IV.B: Queueing in shared memory
- Why the obvious approaches fail: a shared counter of spinners costs two extra atomics per critical section and, worse, every spinner must read it to compute its delay, which on non-distributed-write systems consumes as much bandwidth as polling the lock directly. A naive explicit queue requires locking the enqueue/dequeue operations, which must modify more than one location (even the VAX atomic enqueue/dequeue are slow), giving much worse performance for small critical sections. The notes present the array queue without this motivation. (Section IV.B)
- Anderson's queueing lock requires only one atomic (read-and-increment) per critical section; a releasing processor "taps" the next sequence number, so no read-modify-write is needed to pass control. Table V gives the code with `flags[0] := HAS_LOCK; flags[1..P-1] := MUST_WAIT; queueLast := 0`, lock = `myPlace := ReadAndIncrement(queueLast); while (flags[myPlace mod P] = MUST_WAIT); flags[myPlace mod P] := MUST_WAIT`, unlock = `flags[(myPlace+1) mod P] := HAS_LOCK`. Sequent (Graunke) independently devised a similar algorithm. (Section IV.B, Table V) [Now stated in the note: one up-front atomic and direct handoff to the next flag in the note body (sections Queueing Lock and Queueing Lock Implementation), the Table V code in the body plus the reset-placement callout, and Graunke in the queueing callout; so not beyond the notes.]
- Per-architecture best implementation, which the notes compress to "need cache coherence and separate cache lines": with distributed-write all processors can spin on a single counter and the releaser just writes its sequence number (one bus transaction updates the next processor); with invalidation each flag goes in a separate cache block (two transactions, an invalidation and a read miss, to signal the next); on a multistage network without caches each flag goes in a separate memory module. (Section IV.B)
- A bus-without-coherence caveat: queueing is less valuable there because processors must still poll, swamping the bus; a delay between polls (depending on queue distance and critical-section length) helps, and this is one way to use fewer than P locations, by polling rarely until close to the front. (Section IV.B)
- If there is no atomic read-and-increment the operation can itself be locked; it is only a few instructions so it rarely becomes a bottleneck except with trivial critical sections or bursty arrivals, in which case one of the delay alternatives should guard it. Anderson notes the Symmetry supports an atomic increment but not an atomic read-and-increment (the original value is not saved), which is why his measured queueing lock uses a backoff-protected sequence number. (Section IV.B) [The Symmetry's missing read-and-increment and the backoff-protected sequence number are now stated in the note's queueing callout; locking the operation in general and when it bottlenecks are not.]
- The "parallelism" argument the notes partly capture: because a spinner automatically gets the critical section when its flag is set, the spinner does the time-consuming atomic before the lock is released, cutting the serial handoff work, so throughput can rise as a critical section becomes a bottleneck. (Section IV.B) [Now stated in the note's Anderson callout (section Spinlock Performance Comparisons), so not beyond the notes.]
- Two downsides the notes omit entirely. (1) Preemption: normally if a lock holder is preempted all its spinners wait, but with queueing, preempting any spin-waiting process forces everyone behind it to wait if it reaches the front before being rescheduled, causing lock-step behaviour for small, frequently accessed critical sections (cites Zahorjan et al. on spinning-vs-blocking under preemption). One mitigation: if a process can be warned before preemption, it removes itself from the queue by tapping the next process. (2) Multiple events: queueing makes it hard to wait on several locks at once (a processor can be in only one queue), so it cannot exploit splitting a resource across several locks the way random polling with delays can. (Section IV.B) [Both downsides are now stated in the note's queueing callout; the warned-before-preemption mitigation and the Zahorjan citation are not.]

### IV.C: Measurement results (software alternatives)
- Measurement parameters the notes omit: static and dynamic delays ranged 0 to 15 microseconds; a test-and-set takes about one microsecond on the Symmetry; queueing uses an explicit backoff-after-each-reference lock for its sequence number because the Symmetry lacks atomic read-and-increment. (Section IV.C) [That queueing's sequence number is protected by a backoff lock because the Symmetry lacks read-and-increment is now stated in the note's queueing callout; the delay range and test-and-set cost are not.]
- Fig. 3 is explicitly spin-waiting *overhead* (elapsed minus the ideal curve), not raw time, and it includes spin-on-read for comparison but not spin-on-test-and-set. All five new methods have reasonable performance across the range. The one-processor point is lock latency: queueing has high latency, all others low (queueing would have lower latency with a hardware read-and-increment). (Section IV.C, Fig. 3) [Overhead as elapsed minus ideal and the absence of spin-on-test-and-set are now stated in the note (callout and body); that all five methods do reasonably, that the one-processor point is lock latency, and the hardware read-and-increment remark are not.]
- Results the lecture states: near saturation the static-delay alternatives are worse (delays inappropriate for few spinners); backoff tracks the simple methods by adapting; at high spinner counts backoff is slightly worse than static (some collisions are needed to maintain the right delay); queueing is best at high load by parallelizing handoff; and on the Symmetry's invalidation coherence, delaying after each reference is slightly better than after release. (Section IV.C) [Stated in the note body (section Spinlock Performance Comparisons), so not beyond the notes.]
- Fig. 4 (not in the notes): spin-waiting overhead vs number of static slots. Few slots win with few spinners, many slots win with many; the tradeoff sharpens as the maximum spinner count grows (64 slots has much worse low-load performance than plain spin-on-read, yet may be needed to avoid bad high-load performance). Backoff avoids the tradeoff by doing well in both regimes. (Section IV.C, Fig. 4)
- Fig. 5 (not in the notes): a *burst-arrival* experiment where processors are released from a barrier and each then acquires the lock and bumps a counter, overhead normalized per processor. Here queueing does *not* do well at high processor counts, because its sequence-number lock uses backoff; with two processors arriving together, static mean delay is worst, and all methods are bad because of initial contention. (Section IV.C, Fig. 5) [That queueing does not win at high processor counts in Fig. 5, because of its backoff-protected sequence number, is now stated in the note's callout; the barrier setup and the two-processor result are not.]

## Section V: Hardware solutions
- The whole section is absent from the notes, which stop at software queueing. Anderson first notes a general tradeoff: the best coherence mechanism for spin locks may differ from the best for normal references, and separate-bus solutions (Balance, 4D-MP) add expense of little benefit to applications that do not spin much, and can even hurt latency if the spin-lock bus is slower. (Section V)

### V.A: Multistage interconnection network multiprocessors
- Combining networks (cites Pfister and Norton) merge requests to the same location at a switch; two test-and-sets combine so only one is forwarded and the other returns immediately, and a test-and-set combined with a clear forwards the set while the test-and-set returns having acquired the lock. Combining gives good performance for any number of spinners, but combining switches are more complex and likely slower, so a normal network with backoff or queueing might beat it. (Section V.A)
- Hardware queueing at the memory module: processors issue explicit "enter" and "exit" critical-section instructions and the module maintains per-lock queues, so when an "enter" returns the processor holds the lock with no polling. This eliminates one network round trip relative to software queueing and likely gives better latency despite added module complexity. (Section V.A)
- Goodman et al. proposed holding queue links in the caches themselves (the next processor's name stored in each cache), so release notifies the next processor without going through the memory module, and optionally returning control to software after enqueueing so the processor is separately notified when it reaches the front. (Section V.A)

### V.B: Single bus multiprocessors
- A naive snoopy fix (invalidate only if the lock value actually changes) solves only one problem: on release there is still an invalidation, a cache miss per spinner, and some failing test-and-sets, so time-to-quiesce shrinks but does not vanish and performance still degrades as spinners grow, unlike backoff or queueing. (Section V.B)
- Read-broadcast (cites Segall and Rudolph, and Karlin et al.): a cache controller watching the bus that sees a read for a block it holds invalid takes the data off the bus and validates its own copy, so one read fills all caches and the cascade of read misses is eliminated without full distributed-write. A controller with a pending read that sees another processor granted a read to the same location should just take that returning data. (Section V.B)
- Special test-and-set handling: the cache/bus controller does not commit a test-and-set to the bus while it might fail, and returns immediately without the bus whenever the test-and-set would fail. On a request it checks the cache (read-miss, possibly served by read-broadcast, if absent); if the lock is busy it returns at once; only if free does it arbitrate for the bus, meanwhile monitoring the bus to abort the pending test-and-set if the value is broadcast busy (distributed-write) or convert back to a read if invalidated. Because controllers do not normally know the atomic's type (the ALU does), this needs extra information so the cache can abort pending test-and-sets. Correctness is preserved because the processor's actions are consistent with some serial ordering of atomics. If this does not raise cycle time, it beats software backoff or queueing, which Fig. 3 shows never reach ideal on the Symmetry. (Section V.B)

## Section VI: Conclusions
- The summary conclusions the notes do not state: simple spin-waiting degrades as spinners grow; for machines with only atomic instructions, software queueing and a variant of Ethernet backoff both perform well even for large spinner counts, with backoff better under no contention (simpler, lower latency) and queueing better with waiting processors (parallelized handoff). (Section VI) [Now stated in the note's Anderson callout (section Spinlock Performance Comparisons) and body, so not beyond the notes.]
- Hardware findings: on multistage networks, explicit hardware queueing (at the module or in caches) cuts the handoff time; on shared buses, failing test-and-sets can be handled with no bus traffic given more intelligent snooping. Whether real workloads spin enough to justify such hardware is left as an open question. (Section VI)

## Covered in the notes
- Spinlocks as a basic mutual-exclusion primitive, spinning vs blocking, and spinlocks as a building block for more complex constructs.
- The need for hardware atomic instructions, the broken software-only spinlock, and the test-and-set spinlock that fixes it; the atomicity, mutual-exclusion, and serialized-queueing guarantees of atomics.
- Shared-memory multiprocessors / SMPs, interconnect vs bus, caches hiding memory latency, and no-write / write-through / write-back cache write policies.
- Cache coherence, non-cache-coherent vs cache-coherent architectures, and the write-invalidate vs write-update strategies with their bandwidth / amortization / immediacy tradeoffs.
- Atomics bypassing the cache and triggering coherence traffic regardless of whether the value changes, and why atomics are more expensive on SMP than on a uniprocessor.
- The three spinlock metrics (latency, delay, contention) and that they conflict.
- The test-and-set spinlock (good latency and delay, bad contention) and the test-and-test-and-set / spin-on-read spinlock (okay latency, varying contention by coherence strategy; the O(N^2) write-invalidate release).
- The delay alternatives (delay after release, delay after each reference) and static vs dynamic (perceived-contention) delay, with failed test-and-sets as a contention estimator and the long-critical-section pitfall.
- The queueing (Anderson) lock: the flags array of size N, has_lock/must_wait, read-and-increment for the ticket, private-flag spinning, direct handoff, and the requirements of cache coherence and one element per cache line; its latency/delay/contention profile.
- The performance comparison: queueing best under high load, test-and-test-and-set worst under write-invalidate, static slightly better than dynamic at high load, delay-after-reference slightly better than delay-after-release, and queueing worst under light load because of its latency.

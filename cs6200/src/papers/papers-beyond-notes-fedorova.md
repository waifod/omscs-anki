# Fedorova et al.: everything not in the notes

Everything in the paper that the lecture notes do not state, plus the places where the paper contradicts them; validated against the paper text. The reference point is the lecture notes: the `scheduling.md` note now carries `> **Paper (Fedorova et al.):**` callouts for the lecture-framing discrepancies listed under "Notes vs paper discrepancies", so some gaps below are now annotated in the note. Paper: <https://s3.amazonaws.com/content.udacity-data.com/courses/ud923/references/ud923-fedorova-paper.pdf>.

The paper is a short workshop paper (SIGOPS European Workshop, 2004), six sections plus references, with no printed page numbers in the Udacity extraction; citations below give the section. The authors are Alexandra Fedorova (Harvard and Sun), Christopher Small, Daniel Nussbaum (Sun), and Margo Seltzer (Harvard).

## Abstract and front matter
- The paper's own title in the archival version is "Chip Multithreading Systems Need a New Operating System Scheduler"; the hosted PDF's metadata title is "The Case For Architecture-Aware OS Kernel". (front matter)
- Modern workloads are characterized by frequent branches and control transfers, which can push processor pipeline utilization as low as 19%. (abstract, Section 1)
- CMT (chip multithreading) is defined as a processor architecture combining chip multiprocessing (CMP) and hardware multithreading (MT). (abstract)
- Hardware vendors planned to ship CMT systems "within the next two years", i.e. around 2005; the paper is written ahead of the hardware. (abstract, Section 1)
- The headline simulation result: a CMT-savvy OS scheduler could improve application performance "by a factor of two". The paper is an initial analysis plus a proposed scheduler design, not a finished scheduler. (abstract) [The factor-of-two headline is now stated in the note's Fedorova callout (scheduling.md, section Scheduling for Hyperthreading Platforms); the "initial analysis, not a finished scheduler" point is not.]

## Section 1: Introduction
- Named example workloads with poor CPU pipeline utilization: application servers, web services, and on-line transaction processing (OLTP). They run multiple threads executing short stretches of integer operations with frequent dynamic branches, which hurts cache locality and branch prediction and causes frequent processor stalls. (Section 1)
- Superscalar processors using speculative and out-of-order execution extract instruction-level parallelism (ILP) from scientific workloads but can do little for transaction-processing-like workloads. (Section 1)
- Even some SPEC CPU benchmarks yield pipeline utilizations as low as 19% for some configurations. (Section 1)
- CMP and MT were designed to improve utilization for transaction-processing-like workloads by better supporting thread-level parallelism (TLP). (Section 1)
- Definitions the note does not give: a CMP processor puts multiple cores on one chip so more than one thread is active at a time; an MT processor has multiple sets of registers and thread state and interleaves instructions from different threads, either switching between threads (as often as each cycle) or executing instructions from multiple threads simultaneously if they use different functional units. (Section 1)
- The latency-hiding principle stated in general form: if one thread is blocked on a memory access or other long-latency operation, other threads can make forward progress. (Section 1) [Stated for memory accesses in the note body (scheduling.md, section Hyperthreading); the general form for other long-latency operations is not.]
- Concrete contemporary hardware: IBM's Power4 and Sun's UltraSPARC IV are CMP systems; Intel's hyper-threaded Xeon is an MT system. (Section 1)
- Prior scheduling evidence quantified: an OS scheduler tailored for MT processors produced an average performance improvement of 17% [reference 10]; a naive scheduler can make a multithreaded processor perform worse than a single-threaded one [reference 17]. The note mentions neither figure. (Section 1)
- The scalability thesis: scheduler designs proposed for single-processor multithreaded systems do not scale to the dozens of hardware threads expected on CMT processors, so CMT needs a fundamentally new scheduler design. (Section 1)
- The paper's definition of an ideal scheduler: it assigns threads to processors so as to minimize resource contention and maximize system throughput, scalably, and it must understand how its decisions affect resource contention because contention ultimately determines performance. (Section 1)

## Section 2: Related work
- Prior single-processor MT scheduling algorithms worked by running all combinations of threads that could be co-scheduled, measuring which combinations performed best, and using that data for later decisions. (Section 2)
- These algorithms yielded an average 17% improvement but do not scale: an OLTP workload may involve a hundred threads, and on a CMT system with 16 hardware contexts there are 10^27 combinations to evaluate. (Section 2)
- A second scaling problem: while the scheduler evaluates schedules it is bound to try bad ones, during which the system does not run at best performance. (Section 2)
- The paper's alternative proposal: a scheduler that models relative resource contention from different potential schedules and uses that as the basis for decisions, rather than trying combinations empirically. (Section 2)
- The authors concede modeling resource contention is hard, and suggest a hybrid: use the contention model to shrink the problem space, then fall back on the earlier combination-trying algorithms [references 10-12] for the final decision. (Section 2)

## Section 3: Experimental platform
- The simulator is a CMT toolkit [reference 16] built as a set of extensions to the Simics simulation toolkit [reference 15]. The number of cores per chip and the degree of hardware multithreading are configurable. (Section 3)
- The modeled core uses fine-grained multithreading as proposed by Laudon et al. [reference 6]: usually one, two, four, or eight hardware contexts, each a set of registers and thread state, with the processor interleaving instructions from the threads and switching between contexts on every cycle. This is the detail the lecture compresses into "4-way SMT". (Section 3) [Fine-grained multithreading after Laudon et al. with a context switch every cycle is now stated in the note's Fedorova callout (scheduling.md, section Scheduling with Hardware Counters); the one/two/four/eight context counts are not.]
- When one or more threads are blocked (for example servicing a cache miss) the system keeps switching among the remaining available threads; this is the mechanism behind latency hiding. (Section 3)
- The simulated core is a simple RISC pipeline with one set of functional units (arithmetic unit, load/store unit, etc.), typically configured with four hardware contexts per core. Each core has a single shared TLB and L1 data and instruction caches; the integrated L2 cache is shared by all cores on the chip. (Section 3) [The single set of functional units is now stated in the note's Fedorova callout (scheduling.md, section Scheduling with Hardware Counters); the TLB and cache layout are not.]
- The paper explicitly contrasts its design with SMT. Putting multiple sets of functional units on each core is "termed a simultaneous multithreaded (SMT) system" [references 7, 14]; SMT is more complex and needs more chip real estate. The authors instead leveraged a simple classical RISC core to fit more cores per chip, trading per-core capability for a higher degree of multithreading and higher throughput for multithreaded and multiprogrammed workloads. (Section 3) [SMT as the alternative not taken, and the simple RISC core chosen to fit more cores, are now stated in the note's Fedorova callout (scheduling.md, section Scheduling with Hardware Counters); the complexity/chip real estate reason and the throughput tradeoff are not.]
- Footnote: the modeled pipeline implements result bypassing, which is why ALU instructions have 0 delay latency. (Section 3, footnote 1)

## Section 4: Studying resource contention
- Terminology the note does not use: on a CMT system each hardware context appears to the OS as a logical processor, and a software thread is assigned to a hardware context for the duration of a scheduling time slice. (Section 4)
- The paper scopes its study: there are many categories of contended resources, but this paper focuses on the processor pipeline, because pipeline contention is what distinguishes single-threaded from multithreaded processors. (Section 4)
- The central concept is instruction delay latency: when a thread performs a long-latency operation it is blocked, and subsequent instructions from that thread are delayed until the operation completes; the delay's duration is the instruction delay latency. (Section 4)
- Quantified latencies: ALU instructions have 0 delay latency; a load that hits in the L1 cache has a latency of four cycles; a branch delays the subsequent instruction by two cycles. (Section 4) [Now stated in the note's Fedorova callout (scheduling.md, section Scheduling for Hyperthreading Platforms), so not beyond the notes.]
- The contention rule: a thread dominated by long-delay-latency instructions (memory loads) leaves functional units unused, so contention is low; a thread of strictly ALU operations keeps the pipeline busy and co-scheduled threads suffer. (Section 4)
- The three-system comparison the note does not give: performance on a conventional single-threaded core is the theoretical lower bound; performance on a traditional multiprocessor is the upper bound. When contention is low an MT core approaches MP performance; when contention is high an MT core is no better than a single-threaded processor. (Section 4) [The A/B/C systems and their lower/upper bound roles are now stated in the note's Fedorova callout (scheduling.md, section Scheduling for Hyperthreading Platforms); the low/high contention generalization is not.]
- The worked example uses two workloads (four ALU-only threads with 0-cycle delay latency; four load-only threads with 4-cycle delay latency) across three systems: A, single-threaded; B, four-context multithreaded; C, four-way multiprocessor. (Section 4) [The three systems are now stated in the note's Fedorova callout (scheduling.md, section Scheduling for Hyperthreading Platforms); the ALU-only and load-only workload composition is not.]
- CPU-bound result: A and B perform comparably; C is four times faster because it has four times as many functional units (Figure 1). (Section 4) [Now stated in the note's Fedorova callout (scheduling.md, section Scheduling for Hyperthreading Platforms), except the "four times as many functional units" reason.]
- Memory-bound result: B and C perform comparably and beat A by a factor of four, because B and C overlap the four threads' memory-access latencies (Figure 2), under the assumption that cache hit rate and memory bandwidth are no worse on B than on A or C (footnote 2). (Section 4) [Now stated in the note's Fedorova callout (scheduling.md, section Scheduling for Hyperthreading Platforms), except the footnote 2 assumption.]
- A simulator experiment validated this theory with hand-crafted instruction mixes (strictly ALU for CPU-bound, strictly load for memory-bound), with throughput normalized to System A (Figure 3). (Section 4)
- The paper states instruction mix, and more precisely average instruction delay latency, can serve as a heuristic for approximating a workload's pipeline requirements, and that the scheduler should co-schedule a long-latency-dominated thread with a short-latency, high-functional-unit-demand thread. (Section 4)
- Stated limits of the heuristic: it is only a heuristic, does not model contention for other resources, and so does not always predict performance; in particular it ignores cache contention when threads with large working sets share a processor. (Section 4)
- A second experiment varies a memory-bound thread's working set from 8 bytes to 2MB (Figure 4). The simple heuristic predicts B and C perform comparably, but in the middle of the graph the two curves diverge, caused by the reduced L1 data-cache hit rate on B where four threads share the cache. This is early evidence that cache, not just pipeline, matters. (Section 4)
- The authors say they are currently extending the model to include cache contention and cooperative data sharing. (Section 4)

## Section 5: Scheduling
- Mean CPI, easily determined using hardware counters, "gives us a useful window into the dynamic instruction mix of a workload" and "nicely captures average instruction delay, which can serve as a first approximation for making scheduling decisions." The paper is more confident about CPI than the lecture's "speculates" suggests. (Section 5) [Now stated in the note's Fedorova callout (scheduling.md, section Scheduling with Hardware Counters), so not beyond the notes.]
- Definition of single-threaded CPI the note omits: the CPI a thread would show if it ran on a dedicated processor. Threads with high single-threaded CPI usually have low pipeline resource requirements (blocked on memory or long-latency instructions); threads with low CPI have high resource requirements. (Section 5) [Now stated in the note's Fedorova callout (scheduling.md, section Scheduling with Hardware Counters), so not beyond the notes.]
- The CPI experiment configuration: four cores, four threads per core, a 16-thread workload with four threads each at CPI 1 (CPU-bound), 6, 11, and 16. Four schedules (a)-(d) assign threads to cores as in Table 1. (Section 5) [Now stated in the note body (scheduling.md, section Scheduling with Hardware Counters), so not beyond the notes.]
- The exact per-core assignments (Table 1), which the note shows only as a figure: (a) every core runs 1, 6, 11, 16; (b) cores run 1,6,6,6 / 1,6,11,11 / 1,11,11,16 / 1,16,16,16; (c) cores run 1,1,6,6 / 1,1,6,6 / 11,11,11,11 / 16,16,16,16; (d) cores run 1,1,1,1 / 6,6,6,6 / 11,11,11,11 / 16,16,16,16. (Section 5, Table 1) [Schedule (a), the extremes of (d), and the CPI-1 packing in (c) and (d) are now stated in the note body and the note's Fedorova callout (scheduling.md, sections Scheduling with Hardware Counters and CPI Experiment Results); schedule (b) and the full per-core rows are not.]
- The prediction and reasoning: (a) and (b) should beat (c) and (d) because they pair low-resource (high-CPI) threads with high-resource (low-CPI) threads; in (c) and (d) the CPI-1 threads are packed together (two cores of 1, 1, 6, 6 in (c); one core of 1, 1, 1, 1 in (d)), so those cores are saturated while the cores holding only high-CPI (memory-bound) threads are under-utilized. (Section 5) [Now stated in the note body and the note's Fedorova callout (scheduling.md, section CPI Experiment Results), so not beyond the notes.]
- The quantified result the note states only qualitatively: schedules (a) and (b) perform twice as well as (d) and 1.5 times better than (c) (Figure 5). (Section 5) [Now stated in the note's Fedorova callout (scheduling.md, section CPI Experiment Results), so not beyond the notes.]
- The practicality claims the note does not carry: measured CPI "can vary by an order of magnitude among applications", and "given recent behavior, we can predict the near-term CPI of a thread." On that basis ("given the range and predictability of CPI") the paper concludes that constructing "a scheduler that balances load across CPU cores on a CMT processor should be a simple matter of engineering", and notes it omits elaboration due to space constraints, deferring to future work. (Section 5) [Now stated in the note's Fedorova callout (scheduling.md, section CPI Experiment Results), so not beyond the notes, except the space-constraints deferral.]

## Section 6: Summary
- The summary restates that basing scheduling decisions on CPI "works well for simple workloads" but then lists envisioned limitations, which the note does not reproduce. (Section 6) [The limitations are now stated in the note's Fedorova callout (scheduling.md, section CPI Experiment Results); the "works well for simple workloads" restatement is not.]
- Limitation 1: CPI does not give precise information on the types of instructions executed, so using CPI alone the scheduler cannot tell which threads will compete for other resources such as the cache hierarchy or a floating-point unit. (Section 6) [Now stated in the note's Fedorova callout (scheduling.md, section CPI Experiment Results), except the point that CPI gives no precise information on the types of instructions executed.]
- Limitation 2: when CPI is measured on a busy system it may already be affected by the resource contention present, perturbing the metric. (Section 6) [Now stated in the note's Fedorova callout (scheduling.md, section CPI Experiment Results), so not beyond the notes.]
- The paper's self-summary: CMT systems need new schedulers, a naive scheduler may squander up to half of available application performance, and existing SMT scheduling algorithms do not scale to dozens of threads; the proposed CPI-based scheduler yields a two-fold improvement over a naive one. (Section 6) [The two-fold improvement is now stated in the note's Fedorova callout (scheduling.md, section Scheduling for Hyperthreading Platforms); "squander up to half" and the scalability claim are not.]
- A list of open follow-on topics the authors were investigating, none in the note: inferring single-threaded CPI from CMT CPI; determining the effects of cache contention on co-scheduled throughput; using other workload characteristics such as static instruction mix; studying the nature and dynamics of CPIs in real workloads to see whether CPI is viable for real applications; integrating these ideas with other scheduling policies; and testing on real workloads. (Section 6)

## Covered in the notes
- SMT / hyperthreading as multiple hardware-supported execution contexts on one CPU, with fast context switch and only one thread issuing at a time (lecture treats SMT, hyperthreading, CMT, and hardware multithreading as synonyms).
- The assumptions for the hyperthreading analysis: a thread can issue an instruction every cycle (so max IPC per core is 1), memory access takes four cycles, hardware context switch is instantaneous, two hardware threads.
- Co-scheduling two CPU-bound threads degrades each by a factor of two and leaves the memory system idle; co-scheduling two memory-bound threads wastes CPU cycles; a mix is best and still has minor degradation.
- The need for a fast, hardware-level signal (hardware counters) rather than software sleep-time tracking, because context switch is on the order of cycles.
- Hardware counters: L1/L2/LLC cache misses, instructions retired (IPC computable), power/energy; accessed via oprofile and the Linux perf tool; counters are not uniform across platforms; schedulers combine several counters and platform-specific trained models.
- CPI as a candidate metric, low CPI for CPU-bound and high CPI for memory-bound; no CPI counter on the real processor so a simulator is used; the hope that demonstrating CPI's usefulness pushes hardware vendors to add a CPI counter.
- The CPI experiment (4 cores x 4-way, 16 contexts; CPIs 1, 6, 11, 16; IPC as the metric with max 4) and the conclusion that mixed-CPI schedules utilize the pipeline better.
- The benchmark profiling showing real CPIs cluster together, leading the lecture to doubt CPI as a practical scheduling metric.
- The generalization that schedulers should consider resource contention (pipeline, cache, memory controller, I/O), not just load balancing, and that in follow-on work the last-level cache is the dominant contended resource.

## Notes vs paper discrepancies
Each item was checked against the paper text and at least one third-party source; sources are listed under "Discrepancy sources". Verdicts: LECTURE FRAMING (the note reproduces the lecture and the paper differs; keep the lecture version, annotate with a callout); NOTES WRONG (the note departs from both paper and lecture; fixed in place); NOT A REAL DISCREPANCY.

- The note and lecture call the simulated platform a "4-way SMT". The paper's Section 3 models fine-grained multithreading (Laudon et al.): one set of functional units, context switch every cycle, at most one instruction issued per cycle, and it explicitly names SMT as the alternative it did not build. Annotated with a callout after the "4 cores, 4-way SMT" paragraph.
  - Verdict: LECTURE FRAMING. The lecture (and the course generally) uses SMT and hyperthreading interchangeably; third parties (Tullsen et al., "Exploiting Choice", ISCA 1996; University of Utah CS6810 multithreading slides; the cs.stackexchange consensus) draw the sharper line that fine-grained MT issues from only one thread per cycle while SMT issues from several threads in one cycle and only matters on machines that issue 2+ instructions per cycle. The paper's modeled core (max per-core IPC 1) is fine-grained, not SMT.
- The note says Fedorova "speculates" that CPI is a useful metric and frames high CPI as "takes a lot of cycles, therefore memory bound". The paper is more assertive (CPI "gives us a useful window", "nicely captures average instruction delay") and frames it through single-threaded CPI and resource requirements. Annotated with a callout.
  - Verdict: LECTURE FRAMING. The lecture's "speculates" matches the transcript and is the exam-facing wording; the paper's own text (Section 5) and the follow-on USENIX 2005 paper confirm the stronger phrasing and the single-threaded-CPI reasoning, so both are preserved.
- The note states the CPI-experiment results only qualitatively ("IPC dropped significantly"). The paper gives magnitudes: (a) and (b) are twice (d) and 1.5x (c). Added as a callout, not a correction.
  - Verdict: NOT A REAL DISCREPANCY. The note is not wrong, just less precise; the paper's Figure 5 and third-party summaries (zerofilter's paper notes) supply the numbers, which the callout adds.
- The note says real-workload CPIs cluster in the "2.5 to 4.5" range and concludes CPI "may not be the most instructive metric". The paper never gives that range, says measured CPI "can vary by an order of magnitude among applications", says near-term CPI is predictable, and concludes that "a scheduler that balances load across CPU cores on a CMT processor should be a simple matter of engineering". The paper's stated limitations are different ones (no per-resource detail; perturbation on a busy system). Annotated with a callout.
  - Verdict: LECTURE FRAMING. This is the sharpest divergence: the lecture's "CPI is not useful in practice" takeaway is the opposite of the paper's optimism, and the "2.5 to 4.5" figure is from the lecture graphic. The lecture's conclusion is kept because it is the exam-facing takeaway and it aligns with Fedorova's own later work (USENIX 2005, "Performance of Multithreaded Chip Multiprocessors"), which identifies L2 cache contention rather than pipeline CPI as the dominant effect. The paper's in-scope optimism is recorded in the callout.
- The note's hyperthreading analysis uses a two-hardware-thread example and omits the paper's A/B/C (single-threaded / multithreaded / multiprocessor) framing, the "factor of four" results, and the instruction delay latencies (ALU 0, L1 load 4, branch 2 cycles). Added as a callout.
  - Verdict: NOT A REAL DISCREPANCY. The lecture deliberately simplifies to two hardware threads for teaching; the paper's fuller framing and the delay-latency numbers are additive, supplied by the callout and verified against the paper's Section 4.

Everything in the paper that the lecture notes do not state, plus the places where the paper contradicts or sharpens them; validated against the paper text. The reference point is the lecture notes: the `virtualization.md` note now carries `> **Paper (Rosenblum & Garfinkel):**` callouts for the lecture-framing discrepancies listed under "Notes vs paper discrepancies", so some gaps below are now annotated in the note. Paper: <https://s3.amazonaws.com/content.udacity-data.com/courses/ud923/references/ud923-rosenblum-garfinkel-paper.pdf>.

The paper is a cover feature in IEEE Computer, May 2005, pages 34-43. Authors: Mendel Rosenblum (Stanford and VMware co-founder/chief scientist) and Tal Garfinkel (Stanford). The source text is clean (not OCR), so citations give the section heading; the magazine has no fine-grained page markers beyond the running "May 2005" footers on pages 35-42.

## Opening and "Why the revival?"
- Historical frame: the VMM came into being at the end of the 1960s as a software-abstraction layer partitioning a hardware platform into one or more VMs, each similar enough to the physical machine to run existing software unmodified. (opening)
- The technology flourished briefly, then the 1980s-1990s multitasking operating systems plus falling hardware cost eroded its value; by the late 1980s VMMs were "a historical curiosity." (opening; now stated in the note's Rosenblum & Garfinkel callout, so not beyond the notes)
- The 1990s Stanford revival came from a different problem: massively parallel processing (MPP) machines that were hard to program and could not run existing operating systems. Researchers used VMs to make these machines look similar enough to existing platforms to reuse current operating systems; this project produced the people and ideas behind VMware. The note never gives this MPP origin story. (opening)
- The revival's irony, stated as a thesis: the same cheap-hardware-plus-capable-OS combination that killed VMMs in the 1980s later caused the problems VMMs solve, cheap hardware proliferating underused machines with space and management overhead, and more capable operating systems becoming "fragile and vulnerable." (Why the revival?; now stated in the note's Rosenblum & Garfinkel callout, so not beyond the notes)
- Admins resorted to one-application-per-machine to limit crashes and break-ins, which raised hardware requirements; consolidating those into VMs on a few platforms is what returned the VMM to prominence under the banner of server consolidation and utility computing. (Why the revival?; the one-application-per-machine step and the resulting hardware sprawl are now stated in the note's Rosenblum & Garfinkel callout; the crashes-and-break-ins motive and the server consolidation and utility computing banner remain beyond the notes)
- The forward-looking thesis the note partly echoes: a VMM will be "less a vehicle for multitasking ... and more a solution for security and reliability," and VMMs give OS developers a backward-compatible path to deploy innovation that is no longer practical inside "complex and ossified" operating systems where "innovation moves at a geologic pace." (Why the revival?; the "less a vehicle for multitasking" quote and the "complex and ossified" operating systems are now stated in the note's Rosenblum & Garfinkel callout; the backward-compatible path for deploying OS innovation and the "geologic pace" remark remain beyond the notes)

## Decoupling hardware and software
- Figure 1 is the "classic VMM": a thin layer exporting a VM abstraction that looks enough like the hardware that any software for that hardware runs in the VM. (Decoupling)
- The VMM provides a *uniform* view of hardware, making machines with different I/O subsystems look the same, so a VM can run on any available computer. (Decoupling)
- Encapsulation consequences the note does not enumerate: the VMM can map and remap VMs to hardware at will and migrate them; load balancing and hardware-failure handling become a remap; VMs are easy to replicate; and the VMM can suspend, resume, checkpoint, and roll back VMs, giving a general-purpose *undo* for recovering from crashes or misconfiguration. (Decoupling; migration, copy/clone, and the checkpoint-and-rollback undo are now stated in the note's Benefits of Virtualization section and its Rosenblum & Garfinkel callout; remap at will, load balancing and failure handling as a remap, suspend/resume, and the crash/misconfiguration recovery purpose remain beyond the notes)
- Encapsulation also supports a general mobility model (copy a suspended VM over a network or on removable media). (Decoupling)
- Total mediation of VM-hardware interaction gives strong isolation, which the paper ties to both reliability (a crashing app's OS fault stays contained) and security (a compromised app's attacker is contained to one VM). (Decoupling; containment of bugs and malicious code in isolated VMs is now stated in the note body (virtualization.md, section Benefits of Virtualization); the total-mediation mechanism remains beyond the notes)

## VMM implementation issues (design goals)
- The three central VMM design goals are named: *compatibility* (run legacy software), *performance* (run at close to native speed, a measure of virtualization overhead), and *simplicity* (a VMM failure takes down all its VMs, and secure isolation needs a bug-free VMM). The lecture gives fidelity/performance/safety from Popek-Goldberg instead; this paper's compatibility/performance/simplicity triad is a different, implementation-facing set. (VMM implementation issues)

## CPU virtualization
- Definition: a CPU architecture is *virtualizable* if it supports *direct execution*, running the VM on the real machine while the VMM keeps ultimate control of the CPU. (CPU virtualization)
- Basic direct execution runs both the guest's privileged (kernel) and unprivileged code in the CPU's unprivileged mode while the VMM runs privileged; a guest privileged operation traps into the VMM, which emulates it on the VM state. (CPU virtualization; now stated in the note body (virtualization.md, sections Hardware Protection Levels and Processor Virtualization: hypervisor in ring 0, guest OS pushed to ring 1, trap-and-emulate), so not beyond the notes)
- The disable-interrupts worked example: letting a guest disable interrupts would be unsafe (the VMM could not regain control), so the VMM traps it, records that interrupts are disabled for that VM, and postpones delivering interrupts to the VM until it re-enables them. The note describes the interrupt-flag problem only in the x86-past section, not this VMM handling. (CPU virtualization)
- The paper names the goal precisely: provide trap semantics letting the VMM safely, transparently, and directly use the CPU to run the VM. (CPU virtualization)
- Challenges (the x86 non-virtualizability examples): `POPF` sets/clears the interrupt-disable flag but does not trap in unprivileged mode (it silently ignores the flag change); and unprivileged instructions can read privileged state (reading the code-segment register reveals the real privilege level, which a virtualizable CPU would trap and the VMM would patch). These are the two concrete examples behind the note's "17 instructions" discussion. (CPU virtualization; now stated in the note's Rosenblum & Garfinkel callout in "x86 Virtualization in the Past", so not beyond the notes)
- Techniques: the two most prevalent are *paravirtualization* and *direct execution combined with fast binary translation*. (CPU virtualization; now stated in the note body (virtualization.md, sections Binary Translation and Paravirtualization: the two ways to deal with the non-trapping instructions), so not beyond the notes)
- Paravirtualization detail the note lacks: the VMM builder *redefines the VM interface*, replacing non-virtualizable instructions with efficient virtualizable equivalents; the OS is ported but most applications run unmodified. The canonical example is *Disco*, a VMM for the non-virtualizable MIPS architecture, which turned the MIPS interrupt flag into a special memory location and replaced the MIPS POPF-equivalent and code-segment read with accesses to it, also removing traps and so raising performance. The note attributes paravirtualization to Xen, which this paper does not mention. (CPU virtualization; now stated in the note's Rosenblum & Garfinkel callout in "Paravirtualization", so not beyond the notes)
- Paravirtualization's main drawback: incompatibility, a ported OS, required vendor cooperation, no legacy operating systems, no easy migration of existing machines. Academic projects favored it anyway, because a VMM with both full compatibility and high performance is a significant engineering challenge; VMware, aiming at fast, compatible x86 virtualization, built direct execution plus binary translation instead. (CPU virtualization; now stated in the note's Rosenblum & Garfinkel callout in "Paravirtualization", so not beyond the notes)
- Binary-translation detail: VMware runs kernel code under a binary translator that translates each block into a similar block with problematic instructions (POPF, code-segment reads) replaced, caches it in a *trace cache*, and so translates only on first execution. VMware's translator is simpler than cross-ISA translators because source and target ISAs are nearly identical. The translated output resembles paravirtualized output, except the changes are applied at first execution rather than to OS source. (CPU virtualization; now stated in the note's Rosenblum & Garfinkel callout in "Binary Translation", so not beyond the notes)
- Binary translation is also a *performance* optimization of direct execution: it can eliminate frequently-trapping privileged instructions, cutting overhead that is especially high on deep-pipeline modern x86 CPUs where traps are expensive; once the trace cache is warm, speed is nearly indistinguishable from direct execution. (CPU virtualization; now stated in the note's Rosenblum & Garfinkel callout in "Binary Translation", so not beyond the notes)
- Future support: Intel's Vanderpool and AMD's Pacifica had *announced* hardware support that adds a *new execution mode* (rather than making existing modes virtualizable) to let a VMM use direct execution safely, reducing both the number of traps and their cost; this could enable direct-execution-only VMMs on x86. The paper's forecast that adequate hardware support could make a fully compatible VM abstraction win over the performance benefits of breaking compatibility is the prediction the note's "Hardware Virtualization" section confirms. (CPU virtualization; now stated in the note's Rosenblum & Garfinkel callout in "Hardware Virtualization", so not beyond the notes)

## Memory virtualization
- The paper calls this the traditional implementation technique and names the structure the *shadow page table*: the VMM shadows the VM's memory-management data structure; when the guest OS establishes a mapping, the VMM detects it and installs a corresponding shadow entry pointing to the real machine page, and the hardware uses the shadow table so the VMM always controls each VM's memory. This is the paper's name for the mechanism the note describes. (Memory virtualization; the mechanism is now stated in the note body (virtualization.md, section Memory Virtualization: Full Virtualization) and the name in the note's Rosenblum & Garfinkel callout, so not beyond the notes)
- Overcommit: like an OS's virtual memory, the VMM can page a VM to disk, letting memory allocated to VMs exceed physical memory, so the workload needs less hardware; the VMM dynamically controls each VM's memory. (Memory virtualization; paging a VM to disk so allocated memory exceeds physical memory is now stated in the note's Rosenblum & Garfinkel callout; needing less hardware and dynamic per-VM control remain beyond the notes)
- Challenge and the balloon driver: the VMM must reclaim memory by paging VMs out, but the guest OS has better page-replacement knowledge (for example, it knows a page's creating process exited). VMware ESX Server uses a paravirtualization-like *balloon* process inside the guest: to reclaim memory the VMM "inflates" the balloon, the guest picks pages to give it using its own replacement policy, and the balloon hands them to the VMM. (Memory virtualization; the eviction-knowledge gap, the ESX balloon, inflating it, and the guest choosing pages by its own policy are now stated in the note's Rosenblum & Garfinkel callout; the creating-process-exited example, the paravirtualization-like framing, and the balloon handing pages to the VMM remain beyond the notes)
- Content-based page sharing: a second challenge is redundant copies of identical code/data across VMs; the VMM tracks physical page contents, points shadow page tables at a single shared copy, deallocates redundant copies, and copies-on-write if contents diverge. Example: an x86 box with 30 Windows 2000 VMs but one copy of the Windows kernel in memory. (Memory virtualization; now stated in the note's Rosenblum & Garfinkel callout (all but the "Windows 2000" version detail), so not beyond the notes)
- Future support: operating systems change page tables frequently, so software shadowing is costly; hardware-managed shadow page tables (long present in mainframe virtualization) are a fruitful direction. The note's extended page tables are the realization. The paper also flags cooperative VMM/guest resource management and data-center-level resource management as open research. (Memory virtualization; hardware-managed shadow page tables as a future direction are now stated in the note's Rosenblum & Garfinkel callout; the frequent page-table changes, the mainframe precedent, and the two open research areas remain beyond the notes)

## I/O virtualization
- Mainframe channel history: 30 years prior, IBM mainframe I/O used a channel-based architecture with a separate channel processor, which let the VMM export device access directly to the VM at very low overhead; the VM could read/write the device directly rather than trapping into the VMM. (I/O virtualization; now stated in the note's Rosenblum & Garfinkel callout in "Device Virtualization", so not beyond the notes)
- Challenge: modern x86 has a huge, diverse collection of I/O devices with different programming interfaces, making a VMM I/O layer a huge effort, and some devices (graphics, server network) have extreme performance needs that make low-overhead virtualization critical. (I/O virtualization; now stated in the note's Rosenblum & Garfinkel callout in "Device Virtualization", so not beyond the notes)
- The hosted architecture (Figure 2) is VMware Workstation's design, which the paper credits to its reference [Sugerman et al.]: the virtualization layer uses the *host OS's* device drivers, so a guest disk read/write becomes a host-OS file read/write, and the guest display renders into a host window. The note's hosted-model section uses KVM/QEMU as its example and does not cover this VMware I/O path. (I/O virtualization; now stated in the note's Rosenblum & Garfinkel callout in "Virtualization Models: Hosted", so not beyond the notes)
- Three advantages of the hosted architecture: the VMM installs like an application rather than on bare hardware; it accommodates the rich x86 device diversity via host drivers; and it reuses the host's scheduling and resource management. (I/O virtualization; now stated in the note's Rosenblum & Garfinkel callout in "Virtualization Models: Hosted", so not beyond the notes)
- Disadvantages that drove ESX: hosted I/O overhead is high because each I/O request transfers control to the host and crosses its software layers, unacceptable for high-performance server network/disk; and Windows/Linux lack the resource-management support to give VMs performance isolation and service guarantees. (I/O virtualization; now stated in the note's Rosenblum & Garfinkel callout in "Virtualization Models: Hosted", so not beyond the notes)
- ESX Server runs directly on hardware without a host OS, with sophisticated scheduling and resource management and a highly optimized I/O subsystem; it borrows Linux kernel device drivers to talk directly to devices, feasible because relatively few server network/storage devices are certified. (I/O virtualization; running on bare hardware, borrowing Linux drivers, and the few-certified-devices reason are now stated in the note's Rosenblum & Garfinkel callouts in "Virtualization Models: Hosted" and "Device Virtualization"; the sophisticated scheduling and resource management and the highly optimized I/O subsystem remain beyond the notes)
- Optimized paravirtualized virtual devices: VMware can export highly optimized virtual I/O devices that do not correspond to any real device; like CPU paravirtualization this needs a special guest driver, giving a more virtualization-friendly, lower-overhead I/O interface. This is the paper's version of the note's split-device-driver (front-end/back-end) model. (I/O virtualization; now stated in the note's Rosenblum & Garfinkel callout in "Device Virtualization", so not beyond the notes)
- Future support: discrete I/O devices (the original IBM PC keyboard and IDE controllers) are giving way to channel-like devices (USB, SCSI) that ease virtualization; with hardware support, channel I/O devices could be passed directly to the VM with no VMM trap. For this, devices must know about VMs and support multiple virtual interfaces; DMA-capable devices need *address remapping* so a driver in the VM maps to the right machine memory (per the shadow page tables) and can access only its VM's memory; and the VMM needs efficient routing of device-completion interrupts to the correct VM. The note's "multiqueue devices" and "better interrupt routing" are these predictions realized. (I/O virtualization; channel-like USB/SCSI devices, DMA address remapping, interrupt routing to the right VM, and direct device access with no VMM trap are now stated in the note's Rosenblum & Garfinkel callout in "Device Virtualization"; the discrete IBM PC keyboard/IDE examples and devices knowing about VMs and supporting multiple virtual interfaces remain beyond the notes)

## What's ahead (server side, beyond the machine room, security, software distribution)
- Server side: admins will provision, monitor, and manage thousands of VMs on hundreds of boxes from one console, instantiating servers from templates and treating computers as a generic resource pool; mapping is highly dynamic via hot migration (VMware VMotion), handling hardware failure by remapping and easing maintenance/lease-end/upgrades without service interruption; the future is automated load balancing, failure prediction, and demand-driven VM creation/destruction (VMware Virtual Center is the example). None of this provisioning/management future is in the note. (What's ahead)
- Beyond the machine room (desktop): VMs as a unifying paradigm for desktop management; solving a problem at the VMM layer benefits all software regardless of age or vendor and removes the need for n versions of help-desk/backup software; VMs change user mental models (easy create/copy/share); increased mobility (The Collective, Internet Suspend/Resume) lets a user's whole computing environment migrate over LAN/WAN or ride on a USB drive; dynamic environments need virtual switches, virtual firewalls, and overlay networks. (What's ahead)
- Security improvements: moving host-based security *outside* the VM (alongside but isolated from the OS) gives the same function with much stronger attack resistance; research examples are Livewire (VMM-based intrusion detection) and ReVirt (VMM logging/replay to analyze a break-in); VMMs enable network quarantine, high-assurance systems (NSA NetTop uses VMware to isolate networks of different classifications), and running multiple software stacks at different security levels; VMs are a building block for trusted computing with remote *attestation* (Terra), illustrated by a desktop running low-security Web-browsing, hardened-Linux day-to-day, and high-security mail VMs that servers can attest before granting access; flexible resource management helps resist flash crowds and DDoS. (Security improvements; Livewire intrusion detection, ReVirt break-in analysis, NetTop high-assurance isolation, and Terra attestation are now named in the note's Rosenblum & Garfinkel callout in "Benefits of Virtualization"; moving security outside the VM, ReVirt's logging/replay, NetTop's use of VMware for networks of different classifications, network quarantine, multi-level stacks, the three-VM desktop example, and flash crowd/DDoS resistance remain beyond the notes)
- Software distribution: VMMs let vendors ship entire preconfigured VMs (Oracle distributed 10,000+ copies of its database environment as VMs); the model can serve production, not just demos; VMware ACE lets admins publish and control VMs; The Collective explored bundling applications as *virtual appliances* whose maintainer handles patching; and the model will force software licensing to shift from per-CPU/per-machine toward use-based or sitewide licenses. (Software distribution; distributing preconfigured software stacks as virtual appliances is now stated in the note's Rosenblum & Garfinkel callout in "Benefits of Virtualization"; the Oracle example, production use, VMware ACE, The Collective and maintainer patching, and the licensing shift remain beyond the notes)

## Conclusion and references of note
- Conclusion: the VMM resurgence is fundamentally altering how designers view, manage, and structure complex software environments, and provides a backward-compatible path for innovative OS solutions. (Conclusion)
- References worth knowing: Goldberg's 1974 "Survey of Virtual Machine Research" (the companion to the Popek-Goldberg paper for this lesson); Disco (Bugnion et al., 1997); Denali (Whitaker, Shaw, Gribble); VMware ESX memory management (Waldspurger, the balloon/page-sharing source); VMware Workstation hosted I/O (Sugerman, Venkitachalam, Lim); and the security systems Livewire, ReVirt, and Terra (Garfinkel, Dunlap, and others). (References)

- Bare-metal (type 1) vs hosted (type 2) models, with the note using Xen/ESX and KVM/QEMU as examples (the paper's hosted example is VMware Workstation).
- Direct execution / trap-and-emulate for the CPU, and the interrupt-flag (POPF) problem on pre-2005 x86.
- Binary translation (full virtualization, VMware) with code-fragment caching and kernel-only analysis, and the Rosenblum/Stanford origin.
- Paravirtualization with guest modification and hypercalls (the note attributes it to Xen; the paper uses Disco/MIPS).
- Shadow page tables, write-protecting the guest page table, and that newer hardware reduces memory-virtualization overhead.
- The three device models (passthrough/VMM-bypass, hypervisor-direct, split device driver) and that hardware extensions simplified device virtualization.
- The 2005 AMD Pacifica / Intel Vanderpool hardware virtualization with root/non-root modes, extended page tables, tagged TLBs, multiqueue devices, and interrupt routing.

## Notes vs paper discrepancies
Each item was checked against the paper text and at least one third-party source; sources are listed under "Discrepancy sources". Verdicts follow the fedorova/birrell convention: LECTURE FRAMING (keep the lecture wording, annotate with a callout); NOTES WRONG (fixed in place); NOT A REAL DISCREPANCY.

- The note's hosted-model example is KVM/QEMU; the paper's hosted architecture (Figure 2) is VMware Workstation, with a specific I/O path (guest disk op becomes a host file op) and a three-advantages/two-disadvantages analysis (high hosted I/O overhead; host OSes lacking resource management for performance isolation). Added as a callout in "Virtualization Models: Hosted".
  - Verdict: NOT A REAL DISCREPANCY. Both are valid hosted VMMs; the lecture's KVM example is current and correct, and the callout adds the paper's canonical example and I/O detail. Verified against the paper's I/O section and Figure 2.
- The note attributes paravirtualization to Xen; the paper never mentions Xen and uses Disco (MIPS) as the paravirtualization example. Added as a callout in "Paravirtualization".
  - Verdict: NOT A REAL DISCREPANCY. Xen is the lecture's correct, better-known example and is retained; the callout records that the paper's example is Disco. Verified against the paper's CPU-virtualization section and the Disco reference.
- The note's binary-translation section omits the trace cache, the "translated code resembles paravirtualized output" point, and that binary translation also optimizes direct execution by removing traps. Added as a callout; the Stanford/Rosenblum origin was also added inline to the note.
  - Verdict: NOT A REAL DISCREPANCY. The note is correct but thinner; the callout adds the paper's mechanism. Verified against the paper's CPU-virtualization section.
- The note's memory-virtualization section covers shadow page tables but omits overcommit, the balloon driver, and content-based page sharing. Added as a callout in "Memory Virtualization: Full Virtualization".
  - Verdict: NOT A REAL DISCREPANCY. Additive; verified against the paper's memory-virtualization section and the Waldspurger ESX reference.
- The note's device section omits the mainframe channel history and the paper's framing of the three device approaches (hosted, ESX-with-Linux-drivers, optimized paravirtualized devices). Added as a callout in "Device Virtualization".
  - Verdict: NOT A REAL DISCREPANCY. Additive; verified against the paper's I/O section.
- The note's design goals are implicitly Popek-Goldberg's (fidelity/performance/safety); the paper names a different implementation-facing triad (compatibility/performance/simplicity). This is recorded here but not forced into the note, since the note's goals come from the Popek-Goldberg lecture segment, not this paper.
  - Verdict: NOT A REAL DISCREPANCY. Two different, both-correct framings for two different papers. Verified against the paper's "VMM implementation issues" section.
